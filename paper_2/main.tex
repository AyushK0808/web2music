\begin{abstract}
Generative audio models can create realistic music from text prompts, but
embedding one in an always-on browser extension brings more problems than just
audio quality: extracting a prompt from a webpage, protecting the user's
browser history, and masking the fact that the model runs slowly. We present
Web2Music and identify where our assumptions fall apart. A page descriptor made
up of four signals adds less than a tenth of a second of latency per page.
Our three-part cascade (keyword, zero-shot NLI, large language
model) barely changes accuracy across its confidence
thresholds and, when the middle stage is hosted, does not significantly reduce
off-device disclosure. Fallback and swap enables the user to hear music before
it is generated, even if some cold requests take too long to time out. In a
corpus of websites, requests, and clips, we document five additional negative
results: crossfade that worsens the seam on a large fraction of clips; lost
clip duration; a sensitive content detector that fails to detect most of an
adversarial set of pages; low agreement between annotators on moods; and an
engineered prompt that loses to a mood word alone on nearly every loop metric. 
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
generative audio, context-aware computing, browser extensions, zero-shot
classification, model cascades, on-device inference, ambient interfaces
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

In this study, background music is selected at the beginning of a session and
is ignored for the duration of the session. For example, users are presented
with a low-fidelity playlist that continues uninterrupted for four hours as
they interact with a tax form, a horror wikipedia page, and a birthday
greeting without recognition of changes made. Background music is considered
context-free since selecting background music based on context has
traditionally been dependent upon either a human curator or a library that
addresses all possible contexts. However, text-conditioned generative audio
models potentially eliminate these constraints (\S\ref{sec:related}),
providing the potential to derive the context automatically based on the
activities being performed by the user.

Deriving the appropriate background music is where the technical work occurs
through three constraints that are unrelated to the quality of the audio
generated. The first constraint involves how the prompt is received; i.e., a
web-page is simply a collection of HTML tags representing navigation options,
advertisements, color and content -- not a prompt. The second constraint is
related to classification decisions regarding the user's privacy; e.g., using
an LLM to determine the type of page loaded would provide the most accurate
means of determining whether a user was interacting with their financial
information versus entertainment media. However, utilizing an LLM on each page
would essentially turn a music player into an exfiltration channel for a
history of pages visited by the user. Finally, the third constraint is related
to speed. Since CPU-based processing cannot bridge the gap between the time it
takes to complete cold requests (i.e., requests that require access to
external servers) and the amount of time available before the client times out
due to an inability to receive responses within the allotted timeframe (e.g.,
~2 s for loading a single web page vs. ~30 s for completing a 10-20 s audio
clip), the interaction design must ensure that the process is transparently
invisible.

This paper contributes solutions to each of these constraints. First, this
research developed a four-signal page descriptor (text, sentence embeddings,
page colors, and browsing behavior) whose extraction costs were measured over
105 different real-world sites. An offline ablation test was conducted under
the keyword-stage bounds to evaluate whether the effect of color on the
mood-labels was limited to fewer than 7.5\% of pages and found to be
uninstructive for two auxiliary signals (\S\ref{sec:res-signal-ablation}).

Second, an abstaining three-stage classifier (free keyword heuristic $\to$
zero-shot NLI $\to$ remote LLM) and the finding that, with a hosted cheap
stage, such a cascade redistributes disclosure between providers rather than
reducing it. The thresholds are a privacy mechanism only if the middle stage
is local, which we measure only on an 18-page subset.

Third, a latency-hiding playback design: instant fallback followed by an
abort-on-stale crossfaded swap, with its full latency distribution and failure
modes, including 12 of 144 client timeouts; and loop synthesis from a non-loop
model, bar-snapped chroma self-similarity, a 50 ms crossfade, and a gapless
Ogg/Opus container, evaluated over 141 clips.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\label{sec:recent-systems}
% ===========================================================================

\emph{Generative audio.} MusicGen \cite{musicgen} is a single-stage
transformer language model over discrete EnCodec \cite{encodec} tokens,
text-conditioned and openly deployable, which is why its small variant is
the generator here; MusicLM \cite{musiclm} and AudioLDM \cite{audioldm}
trade fidelity against speed, and AudioGen \cite{audiogen} applies the
same conditioning to environmental sound. All four emit a fixed-length
one-shot excerpt whose end does not join its beginning. AffectMachine-Pop
\cite{affectmachine} is rule-based and truly real time, but takes its
valence--arousal target \cite{russell} from outside rather than from
context.

\emph{Context-aware music and disclosure.} Four recent systems derive
music from a user's situation: MetaBGM \cite{metabgm} from game state,
Discussion Jockey 2 \cite{discussionjockey} from live meeting speech,
Context-AI Tunes \cite{contextaitunes} from a photographed scene plus
self-reported stress, and, on the privacy side, Casper \cite{casper}
sanitises a typed prompt with a client-side cascade before it reaches a
web LLM. FrugalGPT \cite{frugalgpt} established the confidence-gated
cascade pattern this paper's classifier follows, framed as cost and
latency optimisation. Table \ref{tab:litreview} places these against
Web2Music on the properties this paper measures.

Web2Music combines three properties no recent system in Table
\ref{tab:litreview} combines. \emph{Passive context, not a request:}
unlike MetaBGM, Discussion Jockey 2 and Context-AI Tunes, which respond
to something a user is actively doing, Web2Music's context is whatever
page is open, sensed without prompting. \emph{Disclosure as a measured
rate:} every system in Table \ref{tab:litreview} sends its full context
to a cloud model and reports no on-device fraction; Web2Music still
discloses most pages as measured (\S\ref{sec:res-classification}), but
the number is reported rather than assumed away. \emph{Deployment
failure modes as findings:} none of the four context-to-music papers
reviewed reports a latency distribution, cache behaviour, loop-continuity
measurement or adversarial audit. The contribution is accordingly the
deployment measurement -- not a better generator, classifier or cascade,
all three adopted from existing work -- and the finding that several
resulting guarantees do not hold as configured.

\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Gaps}

Prior work leaves four gaps, none about audio quality.
\emph{Deployment-level properties go unmeasured:} prompt cost,
generation-time behaviour and safeguard robustness fall outside the
audio-quality frame prior work uses. \emph{On-device classification is
treated as a cost problem, not a disclosure one:} no work we reviewed
reports an \emph{exposure rate} as a tunable curve. \emph{Context-aware
music systems assume a library:} whether generation beats retrieval on
cost is asserted, not measured. \emph{Generative audio models do not
produce loops:} how much a crossfade removes is not characterised in the
literature we found.

\subsection{Problem statement}

Given a rendered page $p$, produce ambient audio $a(t)$ appropriate to
$p$, subject to \emph{latency} (audible start bounded by the confidence
window plus under a second, independent of generation time),
\emph{disclosure} (text leaving the device is measurable and zero for
sensitive pages), \emph{continuity} (a clip repeats without an audible
seam), and \emph{restraint} (identifiable page classes get no output,
verifiably).

Five verdicts: fallback-then-swap keeps time-to-audio within budget
(\emph{supported}, \S\ref{sec:res-latency}); generation covers a request
space retrieval does not (\emph{argued only under a random-library model},
\S\ref{sec:res-retrieval}); loop synthesis reduces seam discontinuity
(\emph{gapless achieved, seam reduction marginal}, \S\ref{sec:res-loop});
restraint policies fire correctly (\emph{mechanism passes, detection
fails}, \S\ref{sec:res-audit}); the cascade reduces exposure at a
measurable cost (\emph{negative for the hosted backend},
\S\ref{sec:res-classification}).

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 Chrome extension plus one HTTP
audio-generation service. Fig.~\ref{fig:system} follows one page from DOM
to speaker in nine numbered steps and marks the two persistent stores
where they are touched: an IndexedDB vector store on the device and a
Postgres cache index behind the generation service. The division of
labour follows browser security boundaries: the descriptor extractor runs in the content script to reach
the DOM, the classifier runs in the service worker since the content
script's origin is untrusted, and playback and ONNX inference run in an
offscreen document since Manifest V3 forbids audio in a service worker
and a page's CSP blocks WebAssembly there. Three components are network
services: the hosted zero-shot backend, the remote LLM, and the
generation service.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/system.pdf}}
\caption{System architecture diagram.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Page descriptor extraction}

A single function assembles the descriptor from four contextual signals
-- text, a 384-dimensional MiniLM sentence embedding (in-browser ONNX),
colour (an area-weighted HSL histogram over up to 800 elements
\cite{dominantcolour}), and behaviour (scroll/cursor velocity) -- plus
readability \cite{flesch} as an auxiliary feature. The embedding is
searched by cosine similarity against an IndexedDB vector store of prior
pages (step 2 in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}) to flag a revisit, then upserted,
capped at 2,000 entries; this search is the vector-store row of Table
\ref{tab:extraction}. The embedding feeds the vector store only, not the
keyword stage's mood computation (\S\ref{sec:res-signal-ablation}
depends on this). Extraction stays off
the critical path: debounced, idle-scheduled, and re-run on navigation
and DOM mutation.

\subsection{The classifier cascade}

The classifier maps the descriptor onto a 13-way category and 11-way
mood, then a music profile and prompt. Category is a content-topic
label; mood is the affective target that drives the profile, and is
weakly agreed upon among annotators (\S\ref{sec:res-agreement}).

\emph{Keyword stage.} A keyword map gives the category, keyword-only
at this stage; mood blends the keyword score with a colour bias and a
behaviour bias. At confidence $\geq 0.5$ the pipeline stops here at
essentially zero cost; skipped by construction for non-English pages.
\emph{Zero-shot stage.} Scored against thirteen category
hypotheses, accepting a label only if the top score is $\geq 0.45$
\emph{and} the margin over the runner-up is $\geq 0.10$, else abstaining.
A hosted entailment backend transmits page text and is what every
full-scale number here was measured with; a local quantised ONNX backend
transmits nothing, measured only on 18 pages. \emph{LLM stage.} An eight-second-timeout hosted model, falling back to the keyword stage on
failure; the fraction of pages reaching it is the LLM-specific exposure
rate, and total off-device rate adds the pages the zero-shot stage also
received.

A committed mood maps to a music profile and a prompt. Every page the
cascade-ablation harness processes is logged with every stage's output, not
just the winner, so \S\ref{sec:res-classification} can replay any
configuration offline. A newly classified mood must hold for five seconds
before committing -- the largest term in end-to-end latency
(\S\ref{sec:res-e2e-latency}), by design.

\subsection{Generation, loop synthesis and caching}

The generation service validates the request and looks up a Postgres
cache index (steps 5--7 in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}; Supabase-hosted in
production, a local container for every measurement here) keyed on
mood/style/key exactly and energy/valence/arousal/tempo/duration by
bucket, excluding the seed. A hit returns the stored URL and loop point;
a miss generates, post-processes, writes the clip to object storage and
upserts the index row. Any stage failure diverts to a pre-generated
per-mood clip. Requests queue on a single batching worker; a pre-warm
grid of 99 combinations is written into the index ahead of time.

\emph{Loop synthesis.} The generator produces a one-shot clip, not a
loop. Chroma features feed a self-similarity curve \cite{foote} over
candidate loop lengths $\geq 3$ s; the best candidate snaps to a bar
boundary from beat times \cite{librosa} (4/4 assumed), is truncated
there, and its head and tail blended with a 50 ms equal-power crossfade.
Loudness is normalised to $-18$ LUFS \cite{ebu128} and exported as
Ogg/Opus \cite{opus} rather than MP3 -- the container, not the crossfade,
makes the loop gapless, since an MP3 encoder pads every loop audibly. The
service also computes a diagnostic seam discontinuity, the objective
measure in \S\ref{sec:res-loop}.

\subsection{Extension shell and playback}

Playback lives in an offscreen document: two alternating decks over raw
audio buffers honour the loop point, feeding a gain and effects chain.
\emph{Fallback-then-swap} decouples audible start from generation
latency: the client requests the fallback clip and a generation in
parallel; the fallback plays immediately and the generated clip
crossfades in when ready, with an abort controller so a new mood cancels
a stale generation. \emph{Restraint.} Sensitive pages produce silence,
short-circuited before either hosted stage \emph{when the detector flags
them}; an audible active tab mutes, a background one ducks; payment,
banking and browser-internal URLs bypass entirely. \emph{Telemetry} is
a local-only ring buffer, hashed and never transmitted unless explicitly
exported.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation design and provenance}
\label{sec:res-design}

The evaluation is organised as five studies: a systems benchmark, a cascade
ablation, an audio and loop quality study, a within-subjects perceptual
study, and an in-the-wild deployment study. The systems benchmark, the
cascade ablation, the objective half of the audio and loop quality study,
the retrieval baseline and the restraint audit are reported here; the
perceptual study and the in-the-wild study await ethics approval, since
both require exposing human participants to generated audio rather than
replaying logged or synthetic traffic. \emph{Stubs.} Extraction (Table
\ref{tab:extraction}) stubs embedding inference; client latency (Table
\ref{tab:e2e-latency}) stubs the generation service and classify proxy.
Every number is emitted by a named script against a metric registry that
rejects undeclared metrics.

\emph{Corpus.} The cascade-ablation corpus targets 260 pages in seven slices (182
general, 15 vocabulary-mismatch, 15 non-English, 15 mixed-genre, 12
sensitive, 15 near-miss, 6 bypass). Three derived counts do not share one
arithmetic: the extraction snapshot captured 255 of 260; the ablation
snapshot scores 254 with a category label; and the annotator-majority
re-scoring (\S\ref{sec:res-classification}) covers the 208 pages where
both overlap and the vote resolves. \emph{Adversarial slice.} The
restraint audit uses a separate 47-page slice (29 sensitive, 18 not),
hand-written from the detector's own term lists -- white-box test
content, not a naturalistic sample. \emph{Annotators.} Three of the
authors, labelling independently under a preference framing, no
calibration round. \emph{Hardware.} All measurements ran on one CPU
machine except the prompt ablation (\S\ref{sec:res-prompt}), run on a
separate GPU-hosted instance (Apple M4) reached over a tunnel.

\subsection{Annotation agreement and human ceiling}
\label{sec:res-agreement}

Three annotators labelled 255 pages for category (13-way), mood (11-way)
and sensitivity (binary); Table
\ref{tab:agreement} reports Krippendorff's $\alpha$ and a
leave-one-annotator-out ceiling. With three annotators a disagreement
between the other two is a tie, counted as a miss, so the ceiling sits
below $\alpha$ for mood and above it for binary sensitivity (ties are
impossible, and the 87.6 percent skew inflates raw but not
chance-corrected agreement).

\subsection{Which signals earn their cost}
\label{sec:res-signal-ablation}

A per-signal offline ablation over 255 pages replays the keyword stage
with one signal removed at a time. Category never changes (text-only by
construction). For mood, removing colour changes the label on 7.5 percent
of pages, text-alone on 10.2 percent. Behaviour and the embedding are
zero \emph{by construction}, not by finding: the corpus carries only a
fixed synthetic behaviour profile, and the embedding is not an input to
keyword-stage mood at all, so their live contribution is untested.

\subsection{Cost and cache behaviour}
\label{sec:res-cost}

The real corpus escalates past the keyword stage on .762 of pages -- the
same quantity computed slightly differently across three corpus
snapshots (.756--.762), quoted here as .762 throughout -- against .222 on
an earlier 18-page smoke set. At that rate the cascade costs 0.0132 USD
per thousand pages, negligible either way; the cascade is worth building
to control disclosure, not to save money. Cache hit rate is
\emph{modelled}, not measured: 57 percent under a uniform mood
distribution, 83 percent under a skewed one.

\subsection{Generation against retrieval}
\label{sec:res-retrieval}

The request space is $11\times7\times5\times5=1{,}925$ cells. Fig.
\ref{fig:f7} is an analytical model, not a measurement: no library was
built. Under a uniform random-library model a 500-track library covers
under a quarter of the space and roughly 5,000 tracks are needed for 90
percent, falling to 55 percent under skewed commissioning. The claim this
supports is narrow -- coverage grows slowly with library size under this
model -- not that generation covers a space retrieval cannot: a curated
1,925-track library covers 100 percent by construction, and exact-cell
matching overstates what is needed when annotators agree on mood at only
$\alpha=0.195$.

\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music measures where its own deployment assumptions fail. A
four-signal descriptor costs 86 ms median, of which only colour is shown
to move the mood label offline. A three-stage abstaining classifier costs
about 1.3 cents per thousand pages but redistributes disclosure rather
than reducing it, and is dominated by the simpler keyword-to-LLM design
at identical disclosure. Fallback-then-swap decouples a 6.9 s audible
start from a generator that took a median of 139 s on the three requests
that completed, with 12 of 144 requests timing out. Loop synthesis is
gapless but not seamless: the crossfade worsens 41.1 percent of seams and
half the requested audio is discarded to find a loop point. The
sensitive-content detector misses 65.5 percent of an adversarial slice,
so most sensitive pages in it would still reach a hosted model. Three
annotators agree on mood at only $\alpha=0.195$. The engineered prompt
beats neither a bare mood word nor the service's own template on most
objective loop metrics. The central perceptual claim -- that
page-conditioned music fits the page better than page-agnostic music --
remains untested.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

The untested perceptual claim has a concrete instrument, pending ethics
approval: a within-subjects forced-choice comparison of matched against
shuffled audio, counterbalanced, with a Likert fit rating and a
behavioural measure (dwell time, skip/mute) alongside it. A null result
would contradict this paper's premise.

The measurements carry firm limits. Generation latency rests on three
completions; no 28 s cold generation finished inside the client timeout.
Client latency and the extraction embedding are both stubbed, so their
figures are floors. The signal ablation is offline and keyword-stage only,
uninformative by construction for behaviour and the embedding. Mood
classification is unevaluated -- every accuracy figure is for category
while mood drives the music -- and the annotators were three authors with
no calibration round. The retrieval comparison assumes a random library
and unverified generator prompt adherence. Loop-point selection is
optimised on the metric it is evaluated on, at $n=10$. The sensitive
detector's 65.5 percent miss rate, including all non-English cases, is a
defect to fix with a model-based predicate -- plausibly the entailment
stage already in the extension -- before any deployment, not a limitation
to note in passing. The local classification backend that could reduce
rather than redistribute disclosure has been measured only on 18 pages
against a corpus that escalates at .762: the highest-value item
remaining. The larger loop-synthesis opportunity is conditioning the
generator to produce loop-closed material directly; the same
descriptor-to-profile machinery would apply to other continuous contexts
such as documents or code editors.
