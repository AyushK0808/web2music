\begin{abstract}
Text-based music generation models are capable of generating music in
accordance with any text description. Nevertheless, a browser extension that
generates music relevant to the page being viewed by the user needs to obtain
the description from the webpage, to keep the user's browsing history from
leaving the device, and to hide the generator that runs slower than the
browsing process. We propose Web2Music, a Manifest V3 browser extension that
tackles all three challenges. We evaluate the system on 105 live websites, 255
pages of a labeled dataset, 144 generation queries, and 141 generated clips.
The page descriptor has a latency of 86 milliseconds per page, excluding the
embedding inference. The disclosure-aware classifier cascade that consists of
keyword, zero-shot natural language inference, and large language model with
an intermediate stage that operates on-device achieves the same
macro-averaged F1 score of the keyword-to-large-language-model baseline
(0.689) but has 38\% fewer pages sent off-device. With the local gate, we can
trade accuracy for less off-device disclosure, resulting in a fully local
version with a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.594. Fallback-then-swap playback
ensures that the audio becomes perceptible 36 milliseconds after the mood
commitment, regardless of how long it takes to generate, where, in the case of
the Apple M4 machine, generation takes a median of 72 seconds for a cold
28-second segment. The choice of the loop point using a measured seam and not
the one based on similarity in chroma lowers the percentage of seams that have
more than 1 decibel jump in energy from 74\% to 12\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Background music is chosen once, for a session, and then ignored: a lo-fi
playlist runs for four hours across a tax form, a horror wiki and a birthday
message without registering that anything changed. Making it context-aware
has meant recommending \cite{deldjoo} from a library that anticipates every
context. Text-conditioned generative audio models remove that requirement
(\S\ref{sec:related}): once a description can be derived from what the user
is doing, music can be composed for it.

Web2Music does this for the web, and doing it well raises three engineering
problems, none about audio quality. \textbf{The prompt must be derived:} a
page is a DOM of navigation chrome, advertisements, and colour, not a prompt.
\textbf{Classification is a privacy decision:} the most accurate classifier
is a hosted LLM, and calling one on every page would turn a music player into
a browsing-history channel. \textbf{Generation is slower than browsing:} a
cold 28\,s clip takes 72\,s on an Apple M4 and several minutes on a CPU
laptop, against a page load of about two seconds, so the interaction must
make generation time invisible.

Web2Music answers each problem with a component we measure. First, a
four-signal page descriptor (text, sentence embedding, colour, behaviour)
that costs 86\,ms per page excluding embedding inference, 4\% of navigation
time. Second, a disclosure-aware three-stage cascade (keyword $\to$ zero-shot
$\to$ LLM) that treats the share of pages sent off-device as a measured,
tunable quantity: with an on-device middle stage it matches the
keyword-to-LLM baseline's macro-F1 while disclosing 38\% fewer pages, and
moving that stage's gate sets the trade-off anywhere down to a fully local
configuration. Third, fallback-then-swap playback, which makes audio audible
36\,ms after mood commitment regardless of generation time, and loop
synthesis that turns a one-shot generator into gapless loops; choosing the
cut point by measured seam reduces the share of seams with an energy jump
above 1\,dB from 74\% to 12\%. Beyond this system, a hosted middle stage only
changes which provider sees a page, so what matters is whether a local model
is accurate enough; here a quantised one is.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

MusicGen \cite{musicgen} uses a single autoregressive transformer across four
EnCodec \cite{encodec} codebooks with a delay switching between the codebooks
so that a single model generates each stream rather than a chain of models.
It uses a frozen T5 encoder to condition on text, optionally on melody, and
provides checkpoints ranging from 300M to 3.3B parameters. The smallest model
can be run on consumer devices. MusicLM \cite{musiclm} generates
hierarchically from semantic tokens to the SoundStream acoustic tokens
conditioned on the MuLan embeddings of music and text. These generated tokens
remain coherent for many minutes. However, model parameters are not made
available. AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} uses CLAP embeddings to perform latent
diffusion, which allows training on raw audio. Stable Audio
\cite{stableaudio} uses timing embeddings so a single model can generate
audio of arbitrary length. Both models trade autoregressive generation for a
complexity that grows with the number of sampling steps. Text-conditioned
environmental sounds are generated by AudioGen \cite{audiogen}. All of these
models generate a fixed-length segment of audio where the end of the
generated audio is not connected to the start.

LoopGen \cite{loopgen} creates a generative model that can be used to
generate loops without retraining. LoopGen adapts MAGNeT, a masked
non-autoregressive generative model, to generate its own token sequence in a
loop. The end of the sequence is generated with the context of the beginning
of the sequence, and the join between the end and the beginning is generated
rather than later stitched on. While LoopGen leverages the loopable
generation from MAGNeT, the loopable generation does not easily translate to
autoregressive models. Instead, a loop can be cut after generation at a
structural position identified by self-supervised multi-level
representations \cite{buisson} or barwise self-similarity \cite{marmoret}.
AffectMachinePop \cite{affectmachine} is an expert system which can generate
pop music in real time from a desired valence--arousal trajectory
\cite{kang} without the use of a GPU. This allows for direct manipulation of
the perceived affect in the generated music. However, the desired affect
must be provided externally.

There have been three relatively recent approaches that create from user
context. MetaBGM \cite{metabgm} uses an LLM to convert game and interactive
actions into musical descriptions which are then converted into music by a
text-to-music system. The music is maintained across scene changes, but the
full state is sent to a cloud LLM on each request. Discussion Jockey~2
\cite{suzawa} transcribes speech from an online meeting and then converts it
to prompts for MusicGen following the discussion. A user study found a
relaxation effect but the full transcript is sent. Context-AI Tunes
\cite{contextai} uses a combination of a user reported stress level and a
picture of their current context to create a prompt for relaxation music. The
system was tested on 26 users but has active input and the image exits the
device. Casper \cite{casper} is a browser extension that cleanses user input
prior to sending it to a cloud LLM. It uses a local cascade of identifier
rules, NER for locations and names and then an LLM to identify sensitive
information. Casper cleanses rather than creates and has no exposure rate at
the corpus level.

FrugalGPT \cite{frugalgpt} created the confidence-gated cascade. Queries are
attempted using increasingly cheaper LLMs and a learned scorer makes a
decision to either use the response or proceed to the next LLM. It was shown
that performance comparable to GPT-4 can be achieved with up to 98\% less
inference cost. Hybrid LLM \cite{hybridllm} attempts each query with the LLM
predicted to be most appropriate for the query based on difficulty. Up to
40\% fewer large models are used while maintaining output quality.
Teacher-student caching \cite{cachemeifyoucan} uses an online small model
trained on outputs from an LLM to reduce the number of times the LLM is
queried. Agreement-based cascading \cite{agreement} only progresses to the
next model when a group of low cost models do not agree. These systems focus
on reducing cost and latency rather than the content seen by the expensive
model. WeInfer \cite{weinfer} demonstrates the viability of running an LLM
within the browser using WebGPU.

\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Gaps}

Prior work leaves three major research gaps. First, system-level properties
have gone unmeasured: evaluation ends at a system's output, typically audio
quality. Second, cascades have been optimized for cost efficiency rather than
disclosure; no existing cascade permits adjustable exposure. Third, the
paucity of generators that produce loops and the missing seams at
post-processing have gone unmeasured. Web2Music seeks to close these gaps by
systematically deriving music description from any web page passively and
attacking all three at once: it enables the measurement of the entire
pipeline from the DOM to audio, treating exposure as an adjustable rate, and
ensuring all loops are closed with a measurable seam.

\subsection{Problem statement}

For a rendered page $p$, the ambient audio $a(t)$ has four properties.
\textbf{Latency:} audio begins after a set delay that depends on the mood.
\textbf{Disclosure:} text that exits the device is measured; sensitive pages
produce none. \textbf{Continuity:} clips loop without gaps.
\textbf{Restraint:} pages with sensitive categories produce no audio. The
zero-shot and LLM providers, and the key proxy in front of them, receive only
the page title, two-sentence description, and top keywords. The generation
service receives only mood, time of day, and tempo; local generation removes
that channel entirely. Sensitive pages send nothing.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 \cite{mv3} Chrome extension plus one HTTP
generation service. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} follows one page from DOM to speaker
in nine steps and marks the two persistent stores: an IndexedDB vector store
on the device and a Postgres cache index behind the generation service.

\subsection{Page descriptor}
\label{sec:arch-descriptor}

One function assembles the descriptor from four signals: text; a
384-dimensional MiniLM sentence embedding computed in-browser; colour, an
area-weighted HSL histogram over up to 800 elements; and behaviour, scroll and
cursor velocity; plus a readability score. The embedding is matched by cosine
similarity against an IndexedDB store of prior pages to flag revisits, capped
at 2,000 entries.

\subsection{Classifier cascade}
\label{sec:arch-cascade}

The classifier is used to map descriptors to 13 categories and 11 moods, then
maps them respectively to the music profile and prompt. \textbf{Keyword
stage:} at the keyword level, the keyword map is used to find the category,
while moods take the keyword score, colour, and behaviour biases together into
account \cite{jonauskaite}. The cascade also stops at this stage, at zero
cost, when confidence is $\geq 0.5$. \textbf{Zero-shot stage:} the text is
scored versus the thirteen category hypotheses using natural-language
inference \cite{laurer}. Once a label's score attains 0.45 and is at least
0.10 larger than the subsequent highest score, the stage will take a label;
in any other case, it abstains \cite{agreement}. The stage has two
interchangeable back ends: an online hosted bart-large-mnli endpoint, and a
quantised ONNX nli-deberta-v3-xsmall model that runs in the offscreen
document without sending any data. \textbf{LLM stage:} the gpt-oss-20b model
running on Groq is used, with the temperature set to 0. An eight-second
timeout limit is implemented; in case of timeout, the decision would be made
on the basis of keywords. The chosen mood will stay the same for five seconds
in the confidence period before the musical transition to avoid the
perceivable fluctuations due to user scrolling. The externally hosted stages
are available via a proxy that holds the API keys instead of embedding the
credentials into the extension. Every stage's output is logged, so any
configuration can be replayed offline (\S\ref{sec:res-classification}).

\textbf{From mood to prompt.} A committed mood picks a particular row from a
per-mood table, including parameters like tempo span, keys, timbre,
instruments, reverb, and style. Tempo is calculated using interpolation
according to the energy parameter in the chosen range and then tuned with the
help of one of nine page-type tuners and seven time-of-day tuners. Key is
determined by valence, while darker pages are given more reverb. This profile
fills the prompt template ending with loop-based directives (without vocals,
without percussion on the loop point).

\subsection{Generation, loop synthesis and caching}
\label{sec:arch-gen}

The service verifies the query and queries the Postgres cache index (steps
5--7), where the index is exact on the mood, style, and key but also on the
energy, valence, arousal, tempo, and duration buckets. If the match was
successful, the URL and loop point are returned. In case of a miss,
MusicGen-small (300 million parameters, with default sampling) is run on one
batching worker. The worker batches up to four queries within a 150\,ms time
window, and it generates 50 tokens per second of requested audio that is then
processed into an audio clip with index upserting. In case of failure during
any step, the system uses a pre-generated audio clip for the particular mood.

\textbf{Loop synthesis.} Chroma descriptors are employed to create a
self-similarity curve \cite{marmoret} for potential loop segments, which have
a minimum duration of 3 seconds. The best candidate is aligned to a bar
boundary, cut there, and then crossfaded over a period of 50\,ms using an
equal power crossfade. The loudness normalization is performed to
$-18$\,LUFS \cite{ebu128}, after which the clip is exported as an Ogg/Opus
file, which, unlike MP3, adds no padding at every repeat. A measured seam
selector, as discussed in \S\ref{sec:res-loop}, instead examines the same set
of candidates and picks the one whose crossfaded seam has the lowest energy
and spectral discontinuity.

\subsection{Playback and restraint}
\label{sec:arch-playback}

Playback is embedded inside an off-screen document. Looping is performed by
two decks using the raw audio buffer inputs alternatively and then applying
three gain stages (user volume control, tab reduction, and mood fade), where
all the gain stages multiply and not overwrite each other. The
fallback-then-swap strategy involves accessing the fallback clip based on
mood and the next generation simultaneously, playing back the fallback clip
and fading in the new generation based on the equal power curve of the seam
for 3 seconds; the previous generation is killed when there is a new mood.
\textbf{Restraint:} sensitively flagged pages do not have any audio playbacks
and are skipped before the start of either of the two hosted stages. When the
current tab is generating audio, then the player is muted. On the other hand,
background tabs playing audio decrease the gain to 0.1 of the player's
volume. URLs of payment sites, banking websites, and internal browser URLs are
exempted from the process. The system maintains up to 2,000 telemetry events
in SHA-256 hashed form on a local ring buffer, which are never sent out
unless exported.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:res-design}

We report a systems benchmark, a cascade ablation, a loop quality study, and a
restraint audit. Percentiles are nearest-rank. \textbf{Corpus.} 255 real pages
in seven slices (general, vocabulary mismatch, non-English, mixed-genre,
sensitive, near-miss, bypass), 252 of them category-labelled.
\textbf{Adversarial slice.} 47 hand-written pages (29 sensitive, 18 not) built
from the detector's own term lists to target its failure modes.
\textbf{Labels.} Each page carries the category it was collected for; three of
the authors then labelled every page independently, and where their majority
resolves (208 pages) it agrees with that label on 85\%. \textbf{Clips.} The
loop study uses the 141 clips in the service's store, 130 generated and 11
per-mood fallbacks. The systems benchmark ran on a machine with an Intel
i7-1255U and 16\,GB of memory using CPU-only PyTorch; the cold-generation,
duration, and prompt studies ran on an Apple M4 through PyTorch's MPS backend.

\subsection{Annotation agreement}
\label{sec:res-agreement}

Over 255 pages, Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{kalpha} is 0.635 for category,
0.419 for binary sensitivity and 0.195 for mood. Category agreement
approaches but does not reach the 0.667 reliability threshold, so we score
the cascade on category and read its results as tentative. Mood agreement
stays low even after coarsening to valence--arousal quadrants \cite{kang}
($\alpha = 0.121$) or NRC VAD norms \cite{nrcvad} ($\alpha = 0.300$): the
mood of a page is a matter of taste. Web2Music therefore treats mood as a
stylistic mapping from page signals rather than a classification target.

\subsection{Descriptor cost and signal value}
\label{sec:res-descriptor}

On 105 live websites loaded in an automated Chromium browser, descriptor
construction takes a median of 86\,ms against a 1,989\,ms navigation, and
boilerplate removal keeps 80.8\% of visible text. Embedding inference is
stubbed in this benchmark, so 86\,ms is the extraction cost excluding the
model. Text extraction, the colour histogram, and readability take medians of
11.0, 3.9, and 1.5\,ms; most of the remainder, and the whole p95 tail, is the
vector-store search, which is idle-scheduled. An offline ablation on 255
pages replays the keyword stage with one signal removed: removing colour
changes the mood label on 7.5\% of pages, and reducing the descriptor to text
alone changes 10.2\%, the extra 2.7 points coming from the readability term;
both shape about a tenth of mood decisions, which, with no reliable mood
ground truth (\S\ref{sec:res-agreement}), shows influence, not benefit.

\subsection{Classification and disclosure}
\label{sec:res-classification}

Table~\ref{tab:cascade} and Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} give the result. The
single-stage baselines bracket the design space: keyword-only (A1) discloses
nothing at .190 accuracy, and LLM-only (A3) reaches .694 by disclosing every
page. Keyword-to-LLM (A4) comes within .005 of A3's macro-F1 (paired 95\% CI
$[-.041, +.033]$) while keeping 24\% of pages on the device. Every macro-F1
interval is about $\pm.05$ wide, so smaller differences are not resolved by
this corpus.

A local middle stage is what makes the cascade a privacy mechanism. With the
on-device zero-shot model, A6b matches A4's macro-F1 (.689; paired difference
.000, 95\% CI $[-.044, +.042]$) while the fraction of pages leaving the device
falls from .757 to .467, a 38\% reduction. The local model answers in a
median of 390\,ms against 1.74\,s for the hosted one.

The local gate is the disclosure dial. Replaying A6b with the local stage's
gate moved traces the green frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}. Lowering
the score threshold from 0.45 to 0.30 cuts off-device from .467 to .290 at
macro-F1 .675, and opening the gate entirely keeps every page on the device
at .594, level with the hosted zero-shot model alone (A2, .595); A6 at the
default gate reaches only .500 because declined pages fall to a default
category. The gate cells are scored on the corpus they are chosen from, so
they locate the trade-off rather than tune it.

A hosted middle stage only redistributes disclosure. A5 cuts LLM-specific
exposure from .757 to .220, but every page it keeps from the LLM reaches the
hosted zero-shot endpoint instead, so total off-device stays at .757, as the
cascade's structure dictates. A twelve-cell sweep of the hosted gate shows
it: macro-F1 stays within .658--.685 and LLM exposure moves over a
$5.4\times$ range (.106--.576), while total off-device holds at .756--.757 in
every cell. The hosted gate chooses which provider sees a page; only a local
backend changes whether one does.

\subsection{Generation latency and fallback-then-swap}
\label{sec:res-latency}

Table~\ref{tab:latency} and Fig.~\ref{fig:coldgen} show the routes a request
can take. The fallback clip is served in 7\,ms, so the audible start is never
delayed by the generation process. On the Apple M4, all 140 cold generations
of 28 seconds complete, with a median of 72 seconds and a real-time factor of
1.8--2.3 across durations; the generation process itself takes up 71.5
seconds of that median, and every other stage takes under a second. The CPU
laptop is approximately four times slower (real-time factor 8.2--10.1): its
three completed cold generations took a median of 139 seconds, and 12 out of
144 requests, all cold 28-second misses queued one after another on a single
worker, exceeded the 300-second client timeout; the fallback clip played the
whole time. Cache hits on the CPU laptop took 2.09 seconds, almost all of it
spent in the cache lookup, and using pooled Postgres connections lowers that
lookup to 8\,ms.

An automated browser tests the built extension against eleven fixture pages
with generation and classification stubbed out: out of 55 page loads, 54
became audible, with a median latency of 6.86 seconds from navigation. The
first extraction finishes in 0.86 seconds, and the mood then stays stable
throughout the confidence window (median of 6.0 seconds); once the mood
commits, the fallback becomes audible in a median of 36 milliseconds (p95: 54
milliseconds), regardless of what the generator is doing. This is the latency
property of \S\ref{sec:gaps}: the remaining latency is the confidence window,
which is a design parameter, not model latency. The p95 value (11.28 seconds)
is due to one fixture requiring five extraction passes before its mood
commits.

\subsection{Loop quality}
\label{sec:res-loop}

Of the 141 delivered clips, all fall within their duration bounds, and
loudness lands at $-18.09$\,LUFS against the $-18.0$ target. The 50\,ms
crossfade reduces the median seam energy discontinuity from 3.73 to 2.64\,dB
and improves 58.9\% of seams. Delivered loops keep a median 50.3\% of the
requested duration; since the loop repeats gaplessly, this sets the loop
period rather than the playback length, so a 28\,s request repeats about
every 14\,s.

Table~\ref{tab:seam} and Fig.~\ref{fig:seam} compare the two levers on the
same 113 clips. Increasing the crossfade tenfold, from 50 to 500\,ms, lowers
the median energy jump by only 25\%, and 67\% of seams remain above 1\,dB.
The measured-seam selector, which keeps the 50\,ms crossfade and chooses
among the detector's own candidate points, brings the share of seams above
1\,dB down to 12\% and above 3\,dB to 1\% (from 74\% and 36\%), and the
median drops from 2.44 to 0.14\,dB. It makes the seam better on 102 of 113
clips, worse on two, and preserves internal duration retention (.695 vs.\
.690). The selector minimises this same energy jump together with a small
spectral-centroid term, so these numbers show how far the candidates let the
jump fall, not that listeners hear it; the 500\,ms crossfade still gives the
smallest centroid jump.

\subsection{Restraint}
\label{sec:res-restraint}

All fourteen executable restraint policies pass their tests: silence on
sensitive pages \cite{psyguard}, muting and ducking around existing audio,
bypass of payment and browser-internal pages, hashed-URL telemetry, and seven
unit cases. The adversarial slice then attacks the sensitivity predicate.

The term lists are precise, producing only three false positives, all
near-miss academic pages such as a grief-counselling degree. However, a
white-box slice built from the lists' own vocabulary evades them on 19 of 29
sensitive pages, through out-of-list terms (7), euphemisms (4), non-English
text (4), and single ambiguous terms (4). Re-scoring with the entailment
model detects all 29, including the four non-English pages, with scores
between 0.83 and 0.92 (Table~\ref{tab:sensitive}). This run used the hosted
bart-large-mnli backend, which receives every page without a hard-severe
term; in this configuration, the check itself therefore discloses the pages
it is meant to protect. The disclosure property holds only with the on-device
backend, which has not yet been run on this slice. The check also
overtriggers: its eleven false positives include all six plainly benign
pages, such as a Rust tutorial, because the prompt offers only two
hypotheses, crisis or reference discussion, without an ``unrelated'' option,
so the check stays optional until it is re-measured.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

The Web2Music system creates background music that is suitable for the
context of the current page that the user is reading. The pipeline steps of
the system are assessed based on the characteristics outlined in
Section~\ref{sec:gaps}. Running the intermediate step of the pipeline
on-device keeps the keyword-to-LLM macro-F1 score intact and cuts down
off-device page transfers by 38\%, whereas the relevant gate controls the
level of reduction of further information leakage. Playback of audio starts
36 milliseconds after the mood decision, irrespective of the duration of
generation. Choosing the loop point by measured seam also removes most of the
energy jumps at the loop.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

The following stage will be a listening experiment (within-subjects design)
pending ethics clearance to test page-matched audio versus shuffled audio in
terms of forced-choice fit, Likert rating, dwell times, skip rate and mute
rate. Also, the experiment will find out if cleaner seams can be heard and if
the loop period of 14 seconds can cause fatigue. There are three other steps
expected in the engineering process: (1) making A6b and the measured seam
selector the default settings, (2) performing the entailment sensitivity test
on the on-device back end, employing an ``unrelated'' hypothesis, and
re-measuring the two error rates, and (3) conditioning the generator to
create loop-closed content.

The benchmarks do not contain any model inference; the authors are the
annotators, and the gate cells are tested based on the corpus from which they
were extracted. The next set of tests will time the embedding model, use
independent annotators, and use a held-out corpus. An analogous pipeline
could also be used for further continuous contexts such as documents and code
editors.
