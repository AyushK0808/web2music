\begin{abstract}
A browser extension that plays music conditioned on the page being read must
describe the page, keep page text on the device, and hide a slow generator. We present Web2Music, a Manifest V3 Chrome extension that addresses all
three. The page descriptor takes 86\,ms per page without the embedding model. A
three-stage classifier (keywords, an on-device language-inference model, a hosted
large language model) matches the macro-F1 of a keyword-then-LLM baseline on the
255-page development set (0.689) while sending 38\% fewer pages off the device;
the gates are tuned on those pages, with no held-out set. A mood-level
clip becomes audible 36\,ms after the mood is decided. Page-specific music
replaces it when generation finishes, a median of 13\,s for a 15\,s clip on a
consumer GPU, 62\,s on its CPU and 20\,s on an Apple M4, so in a cold-cache
simulation it is heard on 52\%, 34\% and 48\% of visits. Choosing each loop's cut
by its measured seam lowers the share of seams with an energy jump above 1\,dB from
73\% to 9\% on 176 fresh clips. Generated audio is slightly closer to its intended
mood than mismatched audio on three objective scorers; no listening study was run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Background music is chosen once, for a session, and then ignored: a lo-fi
playlist runs for four hours across a tax form, a horror wiki and a birthday
message without registering that anything changed. Making it context-aware
has meant recommending \cite{deldjoo} from a library that anticipates every
context. Text-conditioned generative audio models remove that requirement
(\S\ref{sec:related}): once a description can be derived from what the user
is doing, music can be composed for it.

Web2Music does this for the web. Doing it well raises three engineering
problems, none of them about audio quality. A page is navigation, advertising
and colour, not a prompt, so a description must be derived from it.
Classification is a privacy decision: the most accurate classifier is a hosted
large language model (LLM), and calling one on every page would turn a music
player into a browsing-history channel. And generation is slower than browsing:
a cold 15\,s clip takes 13\,s on a laptop GPU, 20\,s on an Apple M4 and about a
minute on a laptop CPU, against a page load of about two seconds.

This paper gives one measured answer to each. A four-signal page descriptor
(text, sentence embedding, colour, behaviour) costs 86\,ms per page excluding
embedding inference and 360\,ms with it. A three-stage cascade (keywords, a
zero-shot natural-language-inference model, an LLM) treats the share of pages
sent off the device (the \emph{off-device rate}) as a measured, adjustable
quantity: with an on-device middle stage it matches the keyword-then-LLM
baseline's macro-F1 on the development set while disclosing 38\% fewer pages.
Fallback-then-swap playback keeps a sound audible 36\,ms after the mood is
decided whatever the generation time, and choosing each loop's cut by measured
seam cuts the share of seams with an energy jump above 1\,dB from 74\% to 12\%.

What a listener hears falls into three \emph{levels of conditioning}: \textbf{L1},
one of 11 pre-generated mood clips, always available and audible within 36\,ms;
\textbf{L2}, a cached clip generated earlier for a similar music profile; and
\textbf{L3}, a clip generated for this page. We claim music \emph{conditioned on}
the page, not that listeners find it fits; no listening study was run.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

Text-conditioned generators include MusicGen \cite{musicgen} (one autoregressive
transformer over EnCodec \cite{encodec} codebooks, with a 300M-parameter
checkpoint that runs on consumer devices), MusicLM \cite{musiclm}, the
latent-diffusion AudioLDM \cite{audioldm}, Stable Audio \cite{stableaudio} and
AudioGen \cite{audiogen}. All emit a fixed-length segment whose end is not
joined to its start. LoopGen \cite{loopgen} makes a masked non-autoregressive
model generate loops directly; for autoregressive models a loop can instead be
cut after generation at a structural position found by self-supervised
representations \cite{buisson} or barwise self-similarity \cite{marmoret}.
AffectMachinePop \cite{affectmachine} generates pop music in real time from a
valence--arousal trajectory \cite{kang}, but the affect must be supplied.

Context-driven systems derive the prompt from the user's situation, and all send
it off the device: MetaBGM \cite{metabgm} sends game state to a cloud LLM,
Discussion Jockey~2 \cite{suzawa} the meeting transcript, and Context-AI Tunes
\cite{contextai} a photo and a stress level. Casper \cite{casper} is a browser
extension that cleanses input before a cloud LLM, but reports no corpus-level
exposure rate. Model cascades such as FrugalGPT \cite{frugalgpt}, Hybrid LLM
\cite{hybridllm}, teacher-student caching \cite{cachemeifyoucan} and
agreement-based cascading \cite{agreement} cut inference cost and latency, not
what the expensive model sees; WeInfer \cite{weinfer} shows an LLM can run in the
browser with WebGPU.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

Prior work leaves three gaps: system-level properties are unmeasured (evaluation
ends at audio quality); cascades are tuned for cost, not for adjustable
disclosure; and the seams of post-hoc loops are unmeasured. Web2Music is judged
on four properties. \textbf{Latency:} how soon after a mood is decided something
is audible, and how soon page-level audio follows. \textbf{Disclosure:} how much
page text leaves the device, measured rather than assumed. \textbf{Continuity:}
clips loop without gaps. \textbf{Restraint:} sensitive pages produce no audio and
send nothing. The LLM proxy is designed to receive only the page title, a
two-sentence description and keywords, and the generation service only the music
profile; a network audit (\S\ref{sec:res-restraint}) checks both.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 \cite{mv3} Chrome extension plus one HTTP generation
service. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} follows a page from DOM to speaker.

\textbf{Page descriptor.} Four signals: text; a 384-dimensional MiniLM sentence
embedding computed in-browser and matched against an IndexedDB store (2,000
entries) to flag revisits; an area-weighted HSL colour histogram; and scroll and
cursor velocity, plus a readability score.

\textbf{Classifier cascade.} The classifier maps a descriptor to one of 13 content
categories and one of 11 moods. The \emph{keyword stage} stops the cascade at no
cost when confidence is $\geq 0.5$; moods combine the keyword score with colour
and behaviour biases \cite{jonauskaite}. The \emph{zero-shot stage} scores the
text against 13 category hypotheses with natural-language inference
\cite{laurer}; with $s_{(1)}$, $s_{(2)}$ the two highest scores, it takes the top
label only if
\begin{equation}
s_{(1)} \ge \tau \quad\text{and}\quad s_{(1)} - s_{(2)} \ge \delta ,
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
and otherwise abstains \cite{agreement}; the defaults are $\tau = 0.45$,
$\delta = 0.10$. It has a hosted back end (bart-large-mnli) and an on-device one
(quantised nli-deberta-v3-xsmall, ONNX, in the offscreen document). The \emph{LLM
stage} is gpt-oss-20b on Groq at temperature 0 with an 8\,s timeout, after which
keywords decide; a fully local configuration uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct via Ollama.
The LLM stage can be switched off, which makes no LLM request at all
(configuration A6). A mood must hold for 5\,s before the music changes. Hosted
stages sit behind a proxy that holds the keys. The share of pages that leave the
device is
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} P(\text{page } i \text{ reaches an off-device stage}),
\label{eq:disclosure}
\end{equation}
which the gate $(\tau,\delta)$ adjusts. Every stage's output is logged and the
LLM's raw answers are cached, so any configuration can be replayed offline.

A term list flags sensitive pages before either hosted stage, and an optional
on-device entailment check scores the page against three hypotheses (personal
crisis, discussion of a difficult topic, everyday content). A sensitive page
produces no audio and is never sent to the LLM. A committed mood selects a row of
a per-mood table (tempo range, key, timbre, instruments, reverb), tuned by page
type and time of day, which fills a prompt template that ends with loop
directives.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/architecture.png}}
\caption{System architecture of Web2Music.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Generation and loop synthesis.} The service looks the profile up in a
Postgres cache index (exact on mood, style and key; bucketed on energy, valence,
arousal, tempo and duration) and on a miss runs MusicGen-small on one batching
worker; the back end can also be MusicGen-medium or AudioLDM2-music. On any
failure the mood's pre-generated clip is used. Chroma self-similarity
\cite{marmoret} and bar boundaries give candidate loop points (at least 3\,s in);
the default selector cuts at each, applies an equal-power crossfade (250\,ms) and
keeps the candidate with the smallest seam
\begin{equation}
J = |\Delta E| + \lambda\,|\Delta c| ,
\label{eq:seam}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta E$ is the energy jump in dB across the loop, $\Delta c$ the jump in
spectral centroid in Hz and $\lambda = 1/5000$, so the centroid only breaks ties.
The earlier rule, cutting at the chroma-similarity maximum, remains as a switch.
Loudness is normalised to $-18$\,LUFS \cite{ebu128} and the clip is exported as
Ogg/Opus, which adds no padding at repeats.

\textbf{Playback and restraint.} Two alternating decks loop the buffer through
multiplicative gain stages (user volume, tab ducking, mood fade). Fallback-then-swap
plays the mood clip at once and fades in the generated clip over 3\,s when it
arrives. Sensitive pages are skipped before any hosted stage; the player mutes
when the tab itself is playing audio, ducks to 0.1 in background tabs, and skips
payment, banking and browser-internal URLs. Telemetry stays in a local ring buffer
with URLs replaced by a keyed HMAC-SHA-256 digest. In the fully local
configuration (A6) only the music profile leaves the device; in the default
(A6b), also the title, description and keywords of pages the on-device stages
could not decide, sent to the LLM through the proxy.

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:res-design}

Percentiles are nearest-rank; intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap (10,000
resamples). The \emph{development set} is 255 real pages in seven slices (general,
vocabulary mismatch, non-English, mixed-genre, sensitive, near-miss, bypass), 252
category-labelled. Every threshold is chosen on it, so the cascade results are
development-set results and no held-out set was evaluated. The \emph{adversarial
slice} is 47 hand-written pages (29 sensitive, 18 not) built from the detector's
own term lists. Three authors labelled every page independently and the gold
label is their majority, which resolves on 208 pages and agrees with the
collection category on 85\% of them; only category and binary sensitivity are labelled, and
mood is not evaluated. No LLM chose pages, labelled them or judged any output.
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{kalpha} is 0.635 for category (just under the 0.667
reliability threshold, so cascade results are tentative), 0.419 for sensitivity
and 0.195 for mood, which stays low after coarsening to valence--arousal quadrants
\cite{kang} (0.121) or NRC VAD norms \cite{nrcvad} (0.300); mood is therefore a
stylistic mapping, not a classification target. Hardware is an RTX 5060 laptop GPU
(8\,GB), the same machine CPU-only (i7-14650HX), an Apple M4, and an i7-1255U CPU
laptop for the stubbed systems benchmark.

\subsection{Descriptor cost and classification}
\label{sec:res-classification}

On 105 live websites in automated Chromium, descriptor construction takes a
median of 86\,ms against a 1,989\,ms navigation (embedding inference stubbed);
with the real warm MiniLM model on 166 sites the median is 360\,ms (p95 519\,ms),
343\,ms of it the embedding. Of 204 sites attempted, 38 failed to load and are
excluded. Text extraction, colour histogram and readability take medians of 11.0,
3.9 and 1.5\,ms; most of the rest, and the whole p95 tail, is the idle-scheduled
vector-store search. Boilerplate removal keeps 80.8\% of visible text. Removing colour changes the mood label on 7.5\% of pages and reducing
the descriptor to text alone changes 10.2\%; with no reliable mood ground truth
this shows influence, not benefit.

Table~\ref{tab:cascade} and Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} give the cascade results.
Keyword-only (A1) discloses nothing at .190 accuracy and LLM-only (A3) reaches
.694 by disclosing every page. Keyword-to-LLM (A4) is within .005 of A3's
macro-F1 (paired 95\% CI $[-.041, +.033]$) while keeping 24\% of pages on the
device. Every macro-F1 interval is about $\pm.05$ wide. A local middle stage is
what makes the cascade a privacy mechanism: A6b matches A4's macro-F1 (.689;
paired difference .000, CI $[-.044, +.042]$) while off-device pages fall from
.757 to .467, a 38\% reduction, and the local model answers in a median of 390\,ms
against 1.74\,s for the hosted one.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/frontier.pdf}}
\caption{Macro-F1 versus off-device rate.}
\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

The local gate is the disclosure dial: lowering the score threshold from 0.45 to
0.30 cuts off-device from .467 to .290 at macro-F1 .675, and opening the gate
entirely keeps every page on the device at .594, level with the hosted zero-shot
model alone (A2); A6 at the default gate reaches only .500 because declined pages
fall to a default category. The gates are tuned on these pages, so the frontier
locates the trade-off but is not out-of-sample, and the .689 may be optimistic. A
hosted middle stage only redistributes disclosure: A5 cuts LLM exposure from .757
to .220, but the pages it keeps from the LLM reach the hosted zero-shot endpoint
instead, so total off-device stays .757; across a twelve-cell sweep of the hosted
gate it holds at .756--.757 while LLM exposure ranges .106--.576. Only a local
back end changes whether a provider sees a page.

With the real classifier, embedding model and generator, the built extension

model latency.

\subsection{How often generated audio is heard}
\label{sec:res-exposure}

The 36\,ms figure describes the mood clip (L1), not generated audio. Whether a
listener reaches the page-level clip depends on dwell time against generation
time: $P(\mathrm{L3}) = P(T_{\mathrm{gen}} + W < T_{\mathrm{dwell}})$, with
$W = 5$\,s the confidence window. We simulate browsing sessions: moods follow a
Markov chain fitted to the corpus, dwell times are Weibull \cite{liudwell} with
the median swept from 10 to 120\,s (longer for long-form pages), generation times
are drawn from each tier's measured distribution, and there is one worker whose
job is cancelled when the next page needs a different clip. The cache starts empty
each session and the 99-entry pre-warm grid is credited only with its measured hit
rate on real profiles, which is 0 of 235 pages.

The simulation says how often generated audio \emph{could} be heard; it is not a

120\,s; 52\%, 62\% and 68\% on long-form pages). If half the pages hit the
pre-warm grid, which we do not observe, it would fall to 29\% at 30\,s, because a
grid hit is served as L2. A cache persisting across sessions would serve 81\% of
visits as L2, a trivial upper bound since the corpus has only 75 distinct profile
keys.

\subsection{Conditioning fidelity and generator choice}
\label{sec:res-fidelity}

With no listening study we ask a narrower question: does audio generated for a
page agree with its intended mood more than audio for a page of a different mood?
The cascade (with the local Qwen2.5-7B LLM) builds a profile for each of the 234
non-sensitive pages; we generate 702 clips (3 seeds each) on the GPU and compare
each with a \emph{shuffled} control from a page at least two steps away in
valence--arousal space. Scorers are non-generative: Essentia valence/arousal
regressors and the MTG-Jamendo mood tagger; LAION-CLAP, which retrieves a page's
own clip among 10 candidates from its title and lead text (chance 10\%); and
Audiobox Aesthetics for quality.

\subsection{Loop quality}
\label{sec:res-loop}

All 141 delivered clips fall within their duration bounds and land at
$-18.09$\,LUFS. Delivered loops keep a median 50.3\% of the requested duration, so
a 28\,s request repeats about every 14\,s. We compare the two
levers on 113 usable clips (Fig.~\ref{fig:seam}). A tenfold longer crossfade (50 to
500\,ms) lowers the median energy jump by only 25\% and leaves 67\% of seams above 1\,dB; the
measured-seam selector at 50\,ms brings that share to 12\% (and above 3\,dB from
36\% to 1\%), with the median falling from 2.44 to 0.14\,dB, better on 102 of 113
clips and worse on two. Since the selector minimises this energy jump, the numbers
show how far the candidates let it fall, not that listeners hear it.

\caption{Energy jump across loop seams (CDF, 113 clips) by cut method.}
\label{fig:seam}
\end{figure}

A fresh study of 220 seeded clips (11 moods $\times$ 20 seeds) compares the two
rules at the shipped 250\,ms crossfade. Of these, 176 are scoreable (39 have a tail
too short for the crossfade and 5 no loop point). The median $|\Delta E|$ is 2.08\,dB
[1.69, 2.33] for chroma similarity and 0.16\,dB [0.12, 0.23] for the measured seam,
and the share above 1\,dB falls from 72.7\% to 8.5\% (McNemar $p = 2\times10^{-34}$;
71.7\% to 6.2\% on the 145 clips that fit the full crossfade). The seam's
spectral-centroid change, which the selector only uses to break ties, lies within
the clip's own 95th percentile of interior change in 91.4\% [87.7, 95.0] of the
clips. The loops are also \emph{gapless}: rendered through the browser's decoder and a
looping buffer for 10 cycles, 30 of 30 clips show no inserted silence and are
sample-identical from cycle to cycle. Gapless playback is shown; an inaudible seam
is not claimed.

\subsection{Restraint}
\label{sec:res-restraint}

All fourteen executable restraint policies pass their tests: silence on sensitive
pages \cite{psyguard}, muting and ducking, bypass of payment and browser-internal
pages, hashed-URL telemetry, and seven unit cases. The term lists are precise but a
white-box slice built from their own vocabulary evades them on 19 of 29 sensitive
pages (Table~\ref{tab:sensitive}): seven use out-of-list terms, four euphemisms,
four are non-English and four rest on a single ambiguous term. The hosted
entailment model detects all 29, including the four non-English pages, but raises
11 false alarms on 18, among them all six plainly benign pages (e.g.\ a Rust
tutorial), because it offers no ``unrelated'' option, and it discloses every page not caught by a
hard-severe term, so disclosure holds only with the on-device back end. The shipped
check adds the third hypothesis and runs on-device; its recall on real crisis pages
is unmeasured.

\textbf{Network audit.} We recorded every request made by the service worker,
offscreen document, content scripts and pages while the built extension browsed 12
fixture pages (one per mood plus a crisis-support page) three times each. In the
fully local configuration (A6) no request body contained page text; the only
traffic was the generation service, 850 bytes per page. In the default (A6b) page
text left the device on 30 of 36 visits, all to the LLM proxy and nowhere else (5.9
kB per page to the proxy, 0.8 kB to the generation service). The fixtures are not
representative, so this is not the disclosure rate (47\% of development pages); the
audit establishes the destination of the text. No telemetry left the browser.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

An on-device middle stage keeps the keyword-to-LLM macro-F1 while cutting
off-device pages by 38\%, and the fully local configuration sends no page text. A
mood clip is audible 36\,ms after the mood decision, page-level audio is heard on
about half of simulated GPU visits, and measured-seam cuts remove most large
energy jumps. Generated audio leans slightly toward its intended mood on three
objective scorers and loops without gaps; whether listeners hear that fit is the
open question.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

No listening study was run, so we make no claim that listeners find the music fits
the page or that the loops sound seamless; the fidelity measures are proxies with
small effects. The labellers are authors, and there is no held-out test set, so the
cascade's macro-F1 values are in-sample. The exposure results rest on a modelled
dwell time, and the end-to-end and network runs use 11 and 12 fixture pages. The
hardware is one laptop GPU, one Apple M4 and one CPU laptop. The hosted LLM can
change behind the same model name, and the local Qwen2.5-7B configuration gives
different answers. Future work includes a listening study (its protocol is released
with the code) with independent annotators, loop-closed generation, and applying
the pipeline to other continuous contexts such as documents and code editors.
