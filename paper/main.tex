\begin{abstract}
Generative audio models can produce plausible instrumental music from a text
prompt, but placing one inside an always-on consumer application raises three
problems that model quality alone does not solve: the prompt has to be derived
from something, the derivation must not leak the user's browsing history, and
the model is far too slow to sit on the critical path of a page load. We
present Web2Music, a Chrome extension that generates mood-adaptive ambient
music conditioned on the page the user is reading. Three contributions follow
from those constraints. First, a four-signal extraction layer, comprising
article text, a local sentence embedding, dominant page colour, and
scroll and cursor behaviour, produces a compact page descriptor at a median
cost of 86 ms per page in a real renderer across 105 websites. Second, a
three-tier classifier, consisting of a free keyword heuristic, a zero-shot
natural-language-inference tier that may abstain, and a remote large language
model as a last resort, maps that descriptor onto an eleven-way mood and a
thirteen-way content category; because the middle tier abstains rather than
guesses, its two confidence thresholds form a continuous dial between accuracy
and the fraction of pages whose text leaves the device. Third, a
fallback-then-swap playback design hides a generator whose median cold
generation takes 139 s behind a pre-generated per-mood clip served in 7 ms,
while bar-snapped loop-point detection and a 50 ms equal-power crossfade turn
a one-shot generator's output into loopable material. We report a systems
evaluation over 105 websites, 144 generation requests and 141 generated clips,
an adversarial audit of the system's refusal behaviour, and a cost model
showing the cascade classifies a thousand pages for 0.0132 USD. We also report. We also report
three negative results: the crossfade removes a median of only 0.93 dB of seam
energy discontinuity, the delivered clip retains a median of 50.3 percent of
its requested duration, and the sensitive-content detector misses 45.5 percent
of an adversarial crisis slice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Background music is one of the few media people consume while doing something
else. It is chosen once, for a session, and then ignored: a lo-fi playlist runs
for four hours across a tax form, a horror wiki and a birthday message without
ever acknowledging that anything changed. The music is context-free because,
historically, making it context-aware required either a human curator or a
library large enough to cover every context in advance.

Text-conditioned generative audio models remove that constraint in principle.
MusicGen \cite{musicgen} generates instrumental audio from a natural-language
description in a single autoregressive pass over EnCodec \cite{encodec} tokens;
MusicLM \cite{musiclm} does so hierarchically at higher fidelity; AudioGen
\cite{audiogen} and AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} extend the same idea to general
audio. If the description can be derived automatically from what the user is
doing, the music can track it, and the curator is no longer needed.

Deriving it is where the engineering lives. This paper concerns what happens
when a generative audio model is placed behind a consumer browser extension and
made to respond to the open web. Three constraints dominate, and none of them
is about audio quality.

\textbf{The prompt has to come from somewhere.} A web page is not a prompt. It
is a DOM containing navigation chrome, advertisements, a colour scheme, and a
reader whose scroll velocity says something about how they are engaging with
it. Turning that into ``lo-fi study, minimal electronic, deep focus, 88 BPM,
D minor'' is a multi-stage inference problem over noisy, adversarially
formatted input.

\textbf{Classification is a privacy decision.} The most accurate way to
classify a page is to send its text to a large language model. Doing that on
every page turns a music player into a browsing-history exfiltration channel.
The interesting design question is not whether to use an LLM, but how few pages
can be sent to one.

\textbf{The model is too slow to be on the critical path.} We measure a median
of 139 s to generate a 28 s clip on a CPU laptop, against a page load of about
two seconds. No amount of optimisation closes a two-order-of-magnitude gap; the
interaction has to be designed so that the gap is not visible.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item A four-signal page descriptor and its measured cost, characterised over
105 real websites in an instrumented Chromium with per-stage latency and forced
layout counts.

\item An abstaining three-tier classifier that functions as a privacy dial. A
zero-shot NLI tier sits between a free keyword heuristic and a remote LLM and
is permitted to decline when its top score, or its margin over the runner-up,
falls below a threshold. Because it abstains rather than guesses, its two
thresholds trade classification accuracy against \emph{exposure rate}, the
fraction of pages whose text leaves the machine.

\item A latency-hiding playback design, measured end to end on the server side:
instant fallback followed by an abort-on-stale crossfaded swap, reported with
its full latency distribution including failure modes.

\item Loop synthesis from a non-loop model: bar-snapped chroma self-similarity,
a 50 ms equal-power crossfade, and a gapless Ogg/Opus container, evaluated
objectively over 141 generated clips.

\item An evaluation apparatus that constrains overclaiming. Every number is
emitted by a named script against a machine-readable metric registry that
rejects any figure consuming an undeclared metric, and reports artefacts whose
input data does not exist as blocked rather than building them from
placeholders.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section \ref{sec:related}
reviews related work. Section \ref{sec:gaps} states the research gaps and the
problem this system addresses. Section \ref{sec:arch} describes the system
architecture. Section \ref{sec:results} presents and discusses the results.
Sections \ref{sec:conclusion}, \ref{sec:limitations} and \ref{sec:future} give
the conclusion, limitations, and future scope.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Text-conditioned music and audio generation}

MusicGen \cite{musicgen} is the generator used in this work. It models discrete
EnCodec \cite{encodec} audio tokens with a single-stage transformer, avoiding
the cascade of models earlier systems required, and conditions on a free-text
description, which is what makes an automatically derived prompt possible at
all. Jukebox \cite{jukebox} established that raw-audio generative modelling of
music was feasible but at a computational cost far beyond interactive use.
MusicLM \cite{musiclm} produces higher-fidelity long-form audio from text via a
hierarchical sequence-to-sequence formulation, and latent-diffusion approaches
such as AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} and timing-conditioned latent diffusion
\cite{stableaudio} have since improved both quality and speed. AudioGen
\cite{audiogen} applies the same conditioning idea to environmental audio.
AudioCraft \cite{audiocraft} provides the reference implementation for MusicGen
and EnCodec. We use the smallest MusicGen variant because it is the only one in
the family that produces a 28 s clip on a consumer CPU in a time that a
fallback path can plausibly hide.

\subsection{Affective and context-aware music selection}

A separate line of work drives music from an explicit affective specification
rather than free text. AffectMachine-Pop \cite{affectmachine} is a controllable
expert system that generates pop music in real time from a target
valence--arousal point, demonstrating that real-time affective control is
achievable when the generator is rule-based rather than learned. Web2Music
occupies the opposite corner of that trade-off: a learned generator with
greater timbral variety, and an interaction design that pays for its latency.
MusicSense \cite{musicsense} recommends existing tracks from an emotional model
of the text a user is reading and is the closest predecessor in intent; the
difference is that it retrieves from a library where we generate, a choice we
quantify rather than assert in Section \ref{sec:res-retrieval}.

The affective vocabulary itself derives from dimensional models of emotion,
principally Russell's circumplex \cite{russell} and Thayer's arousal model
\cite{thayer}, which we use to place moods in a valence--arousal space and to
map colour and behaviour signals onto shifts within it. Music emotion
recognition, surveyed by Kim et al. \cite{kim2010} and Yang and Chen
\cite{yang2012}, addresses the inverse problem of inferring the mood
\emph{of} a piece of music, using audio and lyrics jointly
\cite{multimodalmood}; the relationship between mood metadata and other
descriptors was characterised early by Hu and Downie \cite{hu2007}. Our
problem is the reverse, and is not strictly a classification problem with a
ground truth, a point we return to in Section \ref{sec:limitations}.

Context-aware computing supplies the framing for a system that reacts to
implicit signals rather than explicit commands \cite{schilit, dey}, and calm
technology \cite{weiser} supplies the design stance: an ambient system's most
important property is often its ability to remain in the periphery. The
psychological literature on background music and cognitive performance is
mixed; a meta-analysis by K\"ampfe et al. \cite{kampfe} finds small and
context-dependent effects, and the irrelevant-speech effect \cite{salame}
suggests why instrumental, non-lyrical material is the safer choice for a
system that plays while people read.

\subsection{Zero-shot classification and model cascades}

The middle tier of our classifier follows the natural-language-inference
formulation of zero-shot classification: a candidate label is turned into a
hypothesis and scored by an entailment model, admitting an unseen label set
without task-specific training. Yin et al. \cite{zeroshotnli} established the
formulation, and work on NLI prompt design for emotion classification
\cite{nliprompts} shows that hypothesis phrasing materially changes accuracy
across corpora, which is why we treat our template as a tunable parameter.
Generative language models offer an alternative zero-shot route
\cite{zeroshotgen}, and that is effectively what our remote tier provides. The
relevant model background is the transformer architecture \cite{attention},
BERT \cite{bert}, its distilled successors \cite{distilbert}, and the sentence
embedding models \cite{sbert, minilm} whose small size makes in-browser
inference practical.

The property we exploit is not accuracy but \emph{abstention}. An entailment
model returns a score per candidate label and cannot invent a label outside the
candidate set, unlike a generative tier, which can return a category that does
not exist. That makes a confidence gate meaningful. Cascading cheap classifiers
in front of expensive ones is a long-established efficiency pattern, from Viola
and Jones \cite{violajones} to recent work on routing queries across language
models of differing cost \cite{frugalgpt}. Our contribution is not the cascade
itself but the observation that when the cascade is deployed on a user's own
machine, the quantity it controls is not latency or cost but disclosure.

\subsection{Extracting signal from a rendered page}

Boilerplate removal, separating article text from navigation, advertising and
footers, is a mature problem; density-based approaches of the kind we implement
were characterised by Kohlsch\"utter et al. \cite{boilerplate}. Readability
scoring follows Flesch \cite{flesch}. Dominant-colour extraction from rendered
content \cite{dominantcolour} gives a cheap, language-independent signal that
survives on pages with little text. Cursor and scroll behaviour carries
information about user engagement and attention in web contexts
\cite{arapakis, cursorcikm}, which motivates our third and fourth signals; we
use them only as a bias term on an already-computed mood distribution rather
than as a primary classifier.

\subsection{Loop synthesis and audio post-processing}

Detecting a repeat point in generated audio is a structural-analysis problem.
Self-similarity over spectral features, introduced for audio segmentation by
Foote \cite{foote}, gives the candidate loop points, and beat tracking
\cite{ellis} as implemented in librosa \cite{librosa} supplies the bar grid we
snap them to. Loudness normalisation follows the integrated-loudness
formulation standardised for broadcast \cite{ebu128}, and the delivered clip is
carried in Opus \cite{opus}, whose container semantics, unlike MP3's, permit
genuinely gapless playback.

\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Gaps}

Reading the two bodies of work above against each other exposes five gaps.

\textbf{G1: generation systems are evaluated as models, not as deployments.}
MusicGen, MusicLM and their successors are evaluated on audio quality and text
adherence given a prompt. How a prompt is obtained in a running application,
what it costs to obtain, and what happens during the tens of seconds the model
takes are outside the frame. There is consequently little published guidance on
placing such a model behind an interactive system.

\textbf{G2: context-aware music systems assume a library.} MusicSense
\cite{musicsense} and comparable recommenders select from a fixed catalogue.
That bounds the achievable specificity to whatever was licensed, and the size
of the request space needed to cover a mood, style, tempo and energy
combination is not usually quantified. Whether generation is worth its cost
relative to retrieval is asserted rather than measured.

\textbf{G3: on-device classification is treated as a latency or cost problem.}
Cascades \cite{violajones, frugalgpt} are motivated by compute and money. When
the cheap tiers run on a user's own machine and the expensive tier is a remote
API, the dominant quantity is neither: it is how much of the user's content
leaves the device. We are not aware of published work that reports an
\emph{exposure rate} as a first-class metric alongside accuracy, or that
exposes it as a tunable operating curve.

\textbf{G4: generative audio models do not produce loops.} A model trained to
produce a fixed-length excerpt gives no guarantee that its end joins its
beginning. Systems that need continuous ambient output must synthesise
loopability after the fact, and the effectiveness of doing so, in particular
how much audio must be discarded and how much discontinuity a crossfade
actually removes, is not characterised in the literature we found.

\textbf{G5: abstention policies in always-on generative systems are
unmeasured.} A system that plays audio over whatever the user reads will
sometimes encounter content over which music is inappropriate. Systems commonly
claim such safeguards; we found no evaluation of one against adversarial input,
including near-miss content that must \emph{not} trigger the safeguard.

\subsection{Problem statement}

Given a rendered web page $p$, produce a continuous ambient audio stream $a(t)$
that is perceived as appropriate to $p$, subject to four constraints:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Latency.} Audio must begin within a few hundred milliseconds of a
committed decision about $p$, despite a generator with a service time of tens
to hundreds of seconds.
\item \textbf{Disclosure.} The fraction of pages whose text is transmitted off
the device must be minimised and must be measurable, and must be zero for
pages classified as sensitive.
\item \textbf{Continuity.} A clip of bounded length must repeat without an
audible seam or container gap for as long as the user remains on $p$.
\item \textbf{Restraint.} For identifiable classes of page, the correct output
is no output, and the system must be verifiable against that requirement.
\end{enumerate}

The claims we test are: (C1) the fallback-then-swap design keeps
time-to-first-audio within the latency constraint while the generator runs;
(C2) the abstaining cascade reduces exposure relative to a keyword-then-LLM
design at a measurable accuracy cost, and the thresholds expose that as a
continuous trade-off; (C3) bar-snapped loop synthesis reduces measured seam
discontinuity; (C4) generation covers a request space that retrieval of a
plausibly sized library does not; and (C5) the restraint policies fire when
they should and not otherwise. C1, C3, C4 and C5 are answered in this paper.
C2 is answered only in part, for reasons stated in Section
\ref{sec:res-classification}.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 Chrome extension plus one HTTP audio-generation
service. Four subsystems, referred to throughout as Features A, B, C and D,
are chained by three versioned handoffs. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the
process topology. The division of labour is dictated by browser security
boundaries as much as by design: Feature A must run in the page's content
script to reach the DOM, Feature B runs in the service worker because the
content script's origin is untrusted, and playback and ONNX inference run in an
offscreen document because Manifest V3 forbids audio in a service worker and a
page's content security policy blocks WebAssembly compilation. Only Feature D
is a network service.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.93\columnwidth]{figures/pipeline.pdf}}
\caption{Process topology and the three handoffs. Everything except Feature D
executes on the user's machine; Feature D is drawn with a dashed border to mark
the only network boundary. The dotted edge carries ONNX inference and IndexedDB
access from the content script to the offscreen document, because a page's
content security policy blocks both in the page context.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature A: page descriptor extraction}

A single function assembles a validated descriptor from six extractors. Text
extraction strips boilerplate by text-density scoring over the DOM and reads
the meta description and document language. A 384-dimension MiniLM sentence
embedding \cite{minilm} is computed in-browser through ONNX in a Web Worker
hosted by the offscreen document, with remote model loading disabled outright.
Colour extraction builds an area-weighted HSL histogram over the computed
background colours of up to 800 sampled elements, yielding dominant hues, a
scalar colour-energy term, and a representative hue, saturation and lightness
triple. A behaviour tracker exposes rolling scroll and cursor velocity in
pixels per second from throttled listeners, capped at ten and twenty events per
second respectively. Readability is a Flesch score \cite{flesch} normalised to
the unit interval. Finally a persistent IndexedDB vector store answers whether
a similar page has been seen before, so that a revisit can reuse a mood rather
than re-run the pipeline; cosine thresholds are 0.95 for a duplicate, 0.85 for
the same topic and 0.70 for a related page.

Two invariants matter. Vectors from different embedding models are never
compared, because a 384-dimension MiniLM vector and a 1536-dimension
alternative describe different spaces, and comparing them returns confident
nonsense rather than an error; records are therefore namespaced by backend,
model and dimensionality. URLs are canonicalised, with tracking parameters and
fragments stripped but meaningful query strings preserved, so that one article
reachable at three URLs is one record.

Extraction is kept off the critical path: it is debounced, scheduled through
the browser's idle callback, and re-run on single-page-application navigation
and throttled DOM mutation.

\subsection{Feature B: the tiered classifier}

Feature B maps the descriptor onto a thirteen-way content category and an
eleven-way mood, then onto a music profile and a text prompt. The cascade is
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/cascade.pdf}}
\caption{The three-tier classifier. Each tier carries its own acceptance
condition; a tier that cannot meet it escalates. Tier 1.5 is an entailment
model and can therefore \emph{abstain}, which is what makes its two thresholds
a continuous dial between accuracy and exposure rather than a fixed operating
point. Tier 2 is the only stage that transmits page text off the device, and it
is unreachable for pages the sensitive check has already short-circuited.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Tier 1, keyword heuristic.} A keyword map over title, description and
cleaned text produces a category. The mood distribution blends the keyword
score with an HSL colour bias, in which saturation and lightness shift valence
and arousal, and a behaviour bias, in which scroll and cursor velocity shift
arousal. If the resulting confidence reaches 0.5 the pipeline stops here, at
essentially zero cost and with nothing leaving the device. The tier is skipped
by construction for non-English pages, which is the single largest known gap in
the design.

\textbf{Tier 1.5, zero-shot NLI with abstention.} On escalation the cleaned
text is scored as an NLI premise against thirteen category hypotheses. The tier
returns a label only if the top score is at least 0.45 \emph{and} its margin
over the runner-up is at least 0.10; otherwise it abstains and escalates. Two
backends exist: a hosted entailment endpoint, and a fully local quantised ONNX
export that runs in the extension's own worker, which makes a zero-exposure
configuration possible at some accuracy cost.

\textbf{Tier 2, remote LLM.} A hosted instruction-tuned model with a structured
JSON response, an eight-second timeout, and a fall-back-to-tier-1 path on any
failure. This is the only tier that transmits page text, and the fraction of
pages reaching it is the exposure rate.

\textbf{Profile and prompt.} The committed mood is mapped to a music profile,
comprising tempo, key, energy, valence, intensity, instrument list, timbre,
reverb, ambience, style and dynamics, and thence to a generator prompt. For the
\emph{focused} mood this yields, in part: ``lo-fi study, minimal electronic,
deep focus. Instruments: piano, minimalist synth, light percussion, bass drone.
Key: D minor, 88 BPM. No vocals. Seamlessly loopable. Instrumental only.''

\textbf{The confidence window.} A newly classified mood does not take effect
immediately; it must remain stable for five seconds before a transition is
committed, so that scrolling through a link-heavy page or a brief detour does
not cause the music to lurch. As Section \ref{sec:res-latency} shows, this is
by far the largest term in the end-to-end wall clock, and it is a design
parameter rather than an implementation limitation.

\subsection{Feature D: generation, loop synthesis and caching}

Feature D is an HTTP service with a five-stage pipeline, shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:featured}: validate, prompt, generate, post-process, cache.
Validation fills every missing field with a default, so a malformed profile
degrades rather than fails.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/featured.pdf}}
\caption{Feature D. The cache is consulted before the generator, and any stage
failure diverts to a pre-generated per-mood clip rather than an error. The D4
box is the loop-synthesis stage that converts a one-shot generator output into
material that repeats; step 3 is where roughly half the generated audio is
discarded, and step 5 is where the container choice, rather than the crossfade,
is what actually makes the loop gapless.}
\label{fig:featured}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Loop synthesis.} The generator produces a one-shot clip, not a loop.
Chroma features are computed and a vectorised self-similarity curve
\cite{foote} is evaluated over candidate loop lengths, excluding anything
shorter than three seconds. The best candidate is snapped to a bar boundary
inferred from beat times \cite{ellis, librosa} under a 4/4 assumption, since
true downbeats are not available. The clip is truncated at the loop point, and
its head and tail are blended with a 50 ms equal-power crossfade so that the
loop is continuous in energy across the seam. Loudness is normalised to
$-18$ LUFS \cite{ebu128} and the clip is exported as Ogg/Opus \cite{opus}
rather than MP3. That last choice is not aesthetic: an MP3 encoder pads the
stream, and the padding is audible as a gap on every loop iteration. The
container, not the crossfade, is what makes the loop gapless. The service also
computes a diagnostic seam discontinuity, the RMS energy and spectral-centroid
differences across the seam, both before and after the crossfade; this is the
objective measure reported in Section \ref{sec:res-loop}.

\textbf{Cache.} Requests are keyed on mood, style and key exactly, on energy,
valence and arousal to one decimal place, on a three-way tempo bucket, a
two-second duration bucket, and the export codec, so that a codec change
invalidates the cache automatically. The generation seed is deliberately
excluded: retries use fresh seeds, and including it would defeat caching
entirely. A pre-warm grid of ninety-nine mood, style and tempo-bucket
combinations is generated ahead of time.

\subsection{Feature C: extension shell and playback}

Playback lives in an offscreen document. Two alternating decks over raw audio
buffer sources, rather than a higher-level player abstraction, are required so
that loop start and end points can honour the detected loop point. They feed
four independent gain stages in series, for crossfade, user volume, ducking and
mood fade, so that un-ducking cannot erase a mood fade in progress, and then an
equaliser, reverb and analyser chain.

\textbf{Fallback-then-swap} is the mechanism that hides generation latency. On
a committed mood the client issues a request for the pre-generated fallback
clip and a generation request in parallel. The fallback clip, correct in mood
and generic in content, begins playing immediately; when generation completes,
the generated clip is crossfaded in on the second deck. One abort controller
per request means a new mood cancels a stale generation rather than letting it
arrive late and overwrite a now-incorrect track.

\textbf{Restraint.} Three behaviours are deliberate refusals to produce output.
Pages classified as sensitive, covering crisis, grief, self-harm and violence,
produce silence, and their text is short-circuited before either LLM tier. If
the active tab is itself audible the extension mutes, and if a background tab
is audible it ducks, so that it never competes with media the user chose.
Payment, banking and checkout pages, and all browser-internal URLs, are
bypassed entirely.

\textbf{Telemetry} is a local-only ring buffer. Every URL is hashed with
SHA-256 before storage and nothing leaves the machine unless the user
explicitly exports it.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation design and provenance}

The evaluation is organised as five studies. S1 is a systems benchmark covering
extraction cost on real sites, generation latency by request class and server
stage, and cost accounting in tokens, bytes and currency. S2 is a
classification accuracy and tier ablation over seven configurations run offline
against a frozen corpus. S3 covers audio and loop quality, objectively over
every clip on disk and subjectively through a forced-choice listening test. S4
is a controlled within-subjects user study with four conditions: silence, a
fixed playlist, a shuffled condition in which the real generator is driven by a
mood drawn from a \emph{different} page, and the adaptive system. S5 is an
in-the-wild deployment. The shuffled condition is what would make the central
perceptual claim falsifiable, since it holds generator, loop processing,
crossfades and latency profile constant and varies only whether the music
matches the page.

S1, the objective half of S3, the retrieval baseline and the restraint audit
have been executed and are reported here. S4 and S5 require institutional
ethics approval that has not been obtained, and no human-subjects data has been
collected. S2 requires an annotated corpus that does not yet exist; what we
report for it is explicitly preliminary and is labelled as such.

Every metric the evaluation may print is declared in a machine-readable
registry with its definition, units, producing script and output field. Each
figure and table generator declares the metric identifiers it consumes, and the
build driver rejects any generator naming an undeclared metric and reports
generators whose input data is absent as blocked, rather than allowing them to
produce an empty or imputed plot. At the time of writing the driver builds
seven of thirteen declared artefacts and blocks six. This apparatus is worth
describing because it is the mechanism that surfaced two of the three negative
results below: a generator refused to print a number its input did not support.

\textbf{Corpus.} The classification corpus is stored as extracted page
descriptors rather than URLs, because live pages change underneath a benchmark
and destroy replication. A capture of 260 pages exists, of which 255 were
captured, three were correctly bypassed and two failed navigation, across seven
deliberately adversarial slices: 182 general, 15 vocabulary-mismatch pages
whose words do not match their category, 15 non-English, 15 mixed-genre, 12
sensitive, 15 sensitive near-miss pages that must \emph{not} trigger silence,
and 6 bypass. No bypass violations and no duplicate content hashes were
recorded. The corpus is not yet annotated.

Four qualifications belong with these tables. First, only three of 144 requests
actually invoked the generator, because the pre-warm grid was already populated
when the run executed; the generation percentiles therefore rest on $n=3$ and
their confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, with a 95 percent interval
on the median of 86.8 s to 207.5 s. Second, there is no cold-cache column: the
run's cold section produced 33 cache hits and no misses, and measuring
cold-cache behaviour requires flushing the cache before the section. Third,
8.3 percent of requests, twelve of 144, hit the client's 300 s timeout; these
are reported as their own row rather than dropped from the percentiles, and a
one-in-twelve timeout rate is itself a finding about the viability of CPU-only
deployment. Fourth, the 2.08 s cache check is anomalous and unexplained: a
lookup against a local database should take single-digit milliseconds, yet it
dominates every cache-hit response and is the largest addressable inefficiency
in the system.

\textbf{Discussion.} The interesting engineering result is not that generation
is slow, which is well known, but that a two-stage fallback-then-swap makes a
139 s generator feel immediate, at the cost of the first seconds of every mood
being generic rather than specific. That cost is nearly invisible, because a
listener who has not heard the specific clip cannot miss it. The corollary is
that the dominant term in the end-to-end wall clock is not the model at all: it
is the five-second confidence window, which we chose, and which exists to stop
the music lurching on a brief detour. Reporting a single time-to-music figure
for this system would therefore be actively misleading, attributing a design
decision about stability to an implementation limitation about speed. We report
the two separately and suggest that any always-on adaptive-media system do the
same.

\subsection{Cost and cache behaviour}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Cost accounting was recomputed over the full S2 corpus (255 of 260 pages; the
two failed captures and three deliberately bypassed internal pages have no
extracted content and are excluded rather than imputed), superseding the
earlier 18-page smoke-set figures. Unit costs are properties of the models and
their pricing rather than of the corpus, so the per-page figures scale with
escalation rate -- and the real corpus escalates far more than the smoke set
implied: 75.7 percent of pages reach the keyword tier's ceiling, against 22.2
percent on the 18-page subset that Table \ref{tab:tiers} still reports pending
the 600-page annotation (D-01/D-02). At the observed rate the cascade sends a
mean of 241.5 LLM tokens per page, 319.1 on the pages that actually escalate,
performs thirteen NLI forward passes per zero-shot invocation and 9.84 per
page on average, and transmits 1642 bytes per page (979 from the LLM tier, 663
from the zero-shot proxy). At the tier-2 provider's published price
(re-verified 2026-08-17, unchanged) this is 0.0132 USD per thousand pages,
rising to 0.0174 USD if every page escalated. 

That figure changes what the cascade contribution claims. Even the pessimistic
number is negligible, so nobody needs a cascade here to save money. The cascade
is worth building because the quantity it controls is how much of the user's
browsing leaves their machine, and that quantity has no price at which it
becomes acceptable to stop measuring. This is the substance of gap G3: the same
mechanism that the efficiency literature motivates by cost is, on a user's own
device, a disclosure control.

Audio cost is dominated by cache behaviour. Over a simulated 200-page browsing
session against the ninety-nine-cell pre-warm grid, the expected cache hit rate
is 57 percent under a uniform mood distribution, 65 percent under mild skew and
83 percent under heavy skew. Which regime real browsing occupies is what the
in-the-wild study would measure, and browsing is almost certainly not uniform.

inter-annotator agreement and therefore no human ceiling to interpret them
against. At that sample size a single page moves accuracy by more than five

Two observations will survive whatever a larger corpus shows, and one of them
is uncomfortable. On this subset the full cascade (A5) is \emph{less} accurate

pages that would otherwise have escalated and answered two of them wrongly.
What it bought in exchange was exposure: A5 transmitted no page text where A4
transmitted 22 percent. Whether that trade is worth making is exactly the
question the threshold sweep is designed to answer, and it cannot be answered
at this sample size. Claim C2 is therefore only partly addressed: the exposure
reduction is mechanically real and visible in the escalation counts, but the
accuracy cost and the shape of the trade-off curve are not yet measured.

The second observation is that the fully local configuration A6b, which uses an

while halving exposure. If that survives a real corpus, a zero-exposure
operating point is available, and the privacy argument becomes considerably
stronger.

\textbf{Discussion.} The generalisable pattern is a free heuristic, a local
model permitted to abstain, and a remote model as a last resort, with the
abstention gates as the dial. Turning them up sends more pages to the remote
model and buys accuracy; turning them down keeps text on the device and costs
accuracy. Because the middle tier is an entailment model rather than a
generative one, it also cannot hallucinate a category outside its candidate
set, which is a robustness property and not merely a cost property. A
generative middle tier would not have it.

Loudness normalisation works exactly as specified, at a median of $-18.09$ LUFS
against a $-18.0$ target, and every clip is duration-compliant. The other two
results are worse than the design assumed, and they qualify C3 rather than
support it.

\textbf{The crossfade helps less than expected, and not always.} The median
paired improvement is 0.93 dB, and the crossfade \emph{worsens} the measured
seam on 41.1 percent of clips. Spectral-centroid discontinuity rises at the
median, from 441 to 557 Hz, while falling at the tail, from 2017 to 1322 Hz:
the crossfade reliably clips the worst seams but perturbs already-good ones. An
earlier run over a subset of 116 clips reported a median improvement of 3.82
dB; the larger sample does not support that figure, and we report the larger
one. A 50 ms window is short relative to the musical events at a bar boundary,
and the obvious hypotheses, a longer or content-adaptive window, or selecting
the loop point to minimise measured seam rather than to maximise chroma
self-similarity, are untested.

\textbf{Half the requested audio is discarded.} The delivered clip retains a
median of 50.3 percent of its requested duration, because the loop point is
chosen for musical self-similarity and everything after it is thrown away.
Requesting 28 s reliably yields about 14 s. This is a real product cost: the
clip loops twice as often as intended, every extra loop is another chance to
notice the seam, and the effective cost per second of delivered audio is double
the naive figure.

Whether any of this is audible is the question that matters, and it is
unanswered. A forced-choice listening harness with twenty-two seeded stimulus
pairs and a scoring script has been built and validated end to end, but only
against simulated listeners, which carries no information about human
perception. No claim about seam audibility is made here.

\textbf{Discussion.} Making a one-shot generator loop requires three things,
and the least obvious one matters most. Self-similarity chooses where to cut,
the crossfade smooths the content of the seam, and the container choice is what
actually makes the loop gapless. Our results suggest the second step is the
weakest link, and that the first step is expensive in a way the design did not
anticipate. This is the concrete content of gap G4: the post-hoc
loopability route works, but at a 50 percent yield and with a smaller
discontinuity reduction than a designer would assume. A generator that could be
conditioned to produce loop-closed material directly would dominate all of it.

The mechanisms pass; the predicate does not. Against a 20-page adversarial
slice, eleven sensitive and nine benign, the sensitive-content detector records
a false-negative rate of 0.455 and a false-positive rate of 0.333. The failures
are systematic rather than noise. Three of the five false negatives are
vocabulary outside the term list: pages on addiction, miscarriage and terminal
illness are not detected at all, because the detector is a term list and those
terms are not on it. One is euphemism, a page that never names the thing it is
about. One is non-English, missed entirely by the same language gate that skips
tier 1. All three false positives are near-misses: a page on grief-counselling
degree programmes, a psychology textbook, and an engineering article using the
word ``trauma'' are all wrongly silenced.

This answers C5 in the negative, and it is the finding with the clearest
consequence for anyone deploying such a system. A keyword list is not a
sensitive-content detector. A system that promises silence on crisis content
and delivers it 55 percent of the time has not implemented the policy it
claims. The architecture is sound, in that the short-circuit is in the right
place and provably fires, but the predicate feeding it is inadequate, and the
remedy is a classifier rather than more keywords. The entailment tier already
loaded for category classification can score a sensitivity hypothesis at near
zero marginal cost.

More generally, this is the substance of gap G5. For an always-on generative
system, the quality of the abstention policy is as much part of the design as
the quality of the generation, and unlike aesthetic quality it is directly
measurable. Measuring it is what produced the most uncomfortable result in this
paper: every mechanism passes its test, while the predicate feeding the most
important mechanism fails on nearly half of an adversarial slice. That gap
between ``the architecture is correct'' and ``the policy is delivered'' is
invisible to any evaluation that tests only the mechanism.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We presented Web2Music, a browser extension that conditions generative ambient
music on the page being read, and reported a systems evaluation of it: a
four-signal page descriptor costing 86 ms at the median across 105 real sites;
a three-tier abstaining classifier that turns confidence thresholds into a
disclosure control and classifies a thousand pages for less than half a cent; a
fallback-then-swap playback design that hides a 139 s generator behind a 7 ms
placeholder; and loop synthesis that makes a one-shot generator's output repeat
without a container gap.

Three results run against the design's own assumptions and are reported as
such. The equal-power crossfade removes a median of only 0.93 dB of seam
discontinuity and worsens the measured seam on 41 percent of clips. Half the
generated audio is discarded to find a loop point, so a 28 s request yields
about 14 s of delivered material. And the sensitive-content detector, whose
refusal mechanism is architecturally correct and fully covered by passing
tests, misses 45 percent of an adversarial crisis slice because its predicate
is a keyword list rather than a classifier.

The claim that page-conditioned music is perceived as fitting the page better
than page-agnostic music remains untested. The study that would test it,
including the shuffled condition that makes it falsifiable, is designed and
instrumented but has not been run.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{No human-subjects data.} The central perceptual claim is untested.
Everything established here is about the system, not about its effect on
people. The shuffled condition that would isolate conditioning from generation
has never been run with a participant, and the forced-choice loop test has been
validated only against simulated listeners.

\textbf{Construct validity of page mood.} What mood a page ``should'' have is a
preference, not a fact. We frame the annotation task as which of the eleven
moods an annotator would least object to hearing while reading the page, and we
hold that any reported accuracy must be printed alongside the inter-annotator
ceiling. No accuracy figure in this paper has that ceiling attached, which is
one reason Section \ref{sec:res-classification} is preliminary.

\textbf{Preliminary classification results.} Table \ref{tab:tiers} rests on 18
pages and one annotator. It shows the structure of the ablation and nothing
more. The threshold sweep that would render the accuracy-exposure trade-off as
a continuous curve, which is the primary evidence C2 requires, has not been run
in any form.

\textbf{English-only.} Tier 1 is skipped by construction on non-English pages,
and the sensitive detector missed the one non-English sensitive page in the
audit. Both are consequences of the same language gate. The pipeline nominally
handles other languages by escalating; whether it handles them well is
unmeasured.

\textbf{Single hardware configuration.} Every latency figure comes from one CPU
laptop. A GPU configuration, on which the fallback-then-swap design plausibly
stops being necessary, and a cache-cold comparison, are both unmeasured.

\textbf{Small samples inside the systems results.} Generation percentiles rest
on three requests, the restraint audit on twenty pages, and the classification
ablation on eighteen. These are stated at each point of use rather than
aggregated away. The concurrency figure measures the request and cache path
rather than generator batching, because every request in that sweep was a cache
hit.

\textbf{Cache state confounds latency and audio together.} A cache hit changes
both the response time and the returned audio, since an identical clip recurs.
Any user study must record cache state per session and should probably force
fresh generation, accepting the latency cost.

\textbf{Ethics and measurement boundaries.} No human-subjects data was
collected; the planned studies require institutional approval that has not been
obtained. Three design decisions are ethically load-bearing: page text is
transmitted off-device only on escalation and never for pages marked sensitive;
telemetry is local-only, URL-hashed at write time and exported only on an
explicit user action; and crisis content produces silence rather than music. We
regard the 45 percent false-negative rate on that last policy as a defect to be
fixed before any deployment to participants, not a limitation to be noted in
passing. Separately, uninstall rate is not measurable in this build, because
the browser deletes extension-local storage on uninstall and the only surviving
hook would require a network call that the local-only guarantee forbids;
voluntary toggle-off is a proxy and not a substitute.

\section{Future Scope}
\label{sec:future}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Complete the perceptual evaluation.} The immediate priority is
institutional ethics approval, followed by the four-condition within-subjects
study and the in-the-wild deployment. The analysis plan is pre-specified:
ordinal outcomes through cumulative-link mixed models with random intercepts
for participant and page, comprehension treated as an equivalence claim rather
than a null result, and multiple-comparison control within a declared
confirmatory family. Sample size is set at 56 to survive attrition at a
small-to-medium effect size.

\textbf{Annotate the corpus and run the threshold sweep.} The 260-page corpus
needs three independent annotators, an agreement coefficient per label set, and
a reported human ceiling. The accuracy-exposure curve obtained by sweeping the
two abstention thresholds is the single most valuable missing artefact, because
it converts a fixed operating point into the dial that gap G3 identifies.

\textbf{Replace the sensitive-content predicate.} A model-based detector,
plausibly the entailment tier already resident in the extension, scoring
sensitivity hypotheses directly, with the adversarial slice expanded well
beyond twenty pages and rebalanced across euphemism, non-English and
outside-vocabulary cases.

\textbf{Improve loop synthesis.} Three cheap experiments follow directly from
Section \ref{sec:res-loop}: a crossfade-window sweep, loop-point selection that
minimises measured seam discontinuity rather than maximising chroma
self-similarity, and a clip-length study asking whether a shorter clip that
loops sooner beats a longer one that arrives later. The larger opportunity is
conditioning the generator to produce loop-closed material directly.

\textbf{Close the measurement gaps.} An end-to-end in-browser latency run to
supply the client-side segments of Fig.~\ref{fig:f1}; a cold-cache re-run to
obtain a genuine cache-miss column and a batching measurement; a GPU
configuration; and an extraction re-run with the real in-browser embedding
rather than a stub. The 2 s cache-check anomaly should be diagnosed first,
since it dominates the common path.

\textbf{Ablate the signals.} The descriptor claims four signals. A
per-signal ablation, removing colour, behaviour and the embedding in turn,
would establish whether the multimodal input earns its complexity. The
instrumentation accepts partial descriptors already, so this is a matter of
running it.

\textbf{Extend beyond English and beyond the page.} The language gate is the
common cause of two separate failures and deserves a dedicated treatment.
Beyond that, the same descriptor-to-profile machinery would apply to other
continuous contexts, such as documents, code editors or reading applications,
where the design questions of latency hiding, disclosure control and abstention
recur unchanged.
