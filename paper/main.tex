\begin{abstract}
A browser extension that plays music suited to the page a person is reading
has to solve three problems: describe the page, keep the page text from
leaving the device, and hide a music generator that is slower than browsing.
We present Web2Music, a Manifest V3 Chrome extension that addresses all three.
The page descriptor takes 86\,ms per page without the embedding model
(360\,ms with the embedding model). A three-stage classifier
(keywords, an on-device language-inference model, a hosted large language
model) matches the macro-averaged F1 of a keyword-then-LLM baseline on the
255-page development set (0.689) while sending 38\% fewer pages off the
device; on a held-out test set of \tbd{S-E: n pages} pages its macro-F1 is
\tbd{S-E: test macro-F1}. Playback starts with a short mood-level clip that
becomes audible 36\,ms after the mood is decided. Music generated for the
specific page replaces it only when generation finishes, which takes a median
of 24\,s on a consumer GPU and 72\,s on an Apple
M4 for a 28\,s clip; in a browsing simulation the generated audio is heard on
28\% of visits with the GPU and 21\% with the M4 (28\,s clips, median dwell 30\,s).
Choosing each loop's cut point by its measured seam lowers the share of
seams with an energy jump above 1\,dB from 74\% to 12\%. Fit to the page is
evaluated with objective conditioning-fidelity measures, not listener ratings;
no listening study was run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Background music is chosen once, for a session, and then ignored: a lo-fi
playlist runs for four hours across a tax form, a horror wiki and a birthday
message without registering that anything changed. Making it context-aware
has meant recommending \cite{deldjoo} from a library that anticipates every
context. Text-conditioned generative audio models remove that requirement
(\S\ref{sec:related}): once a description can be derived from what the user
is doing, music can be composed for it.

Web2Music does this for the web. Doing it well raises three engineering
problems, none of them about audio quality.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The prompt must be derived.} A page is navigation, advertising
and colour, not a prompt.
\item \textbf{Classification is a privacy decision.} The most accurate
classifier is a hosted large language model (LLM), and calling one on every
page would turn a music player into a browsing-history channel.
\item \textbf{Generation is slower than browsing.} A cold 28\,s clip takes
72\,s on an Apple M4 and several minutes on a CPU laptop, against a page load
of about two seconds.
\end{enumerate}

This paper contributes one measured answer to each.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A four-signal page descriptor (text, sentence embedding, colour,
behaviour) that costs 86\,ms per page excluding embedding inference.
\item A three-stage cascade (keywords, then a zero-shot natural-language-inference
model, then an LLM) that treats the share of pages sent off the device (the
\emph{off-device rate}) as a measured, adjustable quantity. With an on-device
middle stage it matches the keyword-then-LLM baseline's macro-F1 (the mean over
categories of each category's F1) on the development set while disclosing 38\%
fewer pages, and the stage's gate moves the trade-off down to a fully local
configuration.
\item Fallback-then-swap playback, which keeps a sound audible 36\,ms after
the mood is decided whatever the generation time, and loop synthesis that turns a
one-shot generator into gapless loops; choosing the cut by measured seam cuts the
share of seams with an energy jump above 1\,dB from 74\% to 12\%.
\end{enumerate}

Because generation is slow, what a listener hears falls into three
\emph{levels of conditioning}. \textbf{L1} is a mood-level clip, one of 11
pre-generated clips, one per mood; it is always available. \textbf{L2} is a
profile-level clip from the cache, generated earlier for a similar music
profile (mood, tempo bucket, energy). \textbf{L3} is a page-level clip,
generated for this page. L1 is audible within 36\,ms; L2 and L3 take longer, and
Section~\ref{sec:res-exposure} measures how often a listener reaches them. This
paper claims music \emph{conditioned on} the page. It does not claim that
listeners find the music fits the page; no listening study was run.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

MusicGen \cite{musicgen} uses a single autoregressive transformer across four
EnCodec \cite{encodec} codebooks with a delay switching between the codebooks
so that a single model generates each stream rather than a chain of models.
It uses a frozen T5 encoder to condition on text, optionally on melody, and
provides checkpoints ranging from 300M to 3.3B parameters. The smallest model
can be run on consumer devices. MusicLM \cite{musiclm} generates
hierarchically from semantic tokens to the SoundStream acoustic tokens
conditioned on the MuLan embeddings of music and text. These generated tokens
remain coherent for many minutes. However, model parameters are not made
available. AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} uses CLAP embeddings to perform latent
diffusion, which allows training on raw audio. Stable Audio
\cite{stableaudio} uses timing embeddings so a single model can generate
audio of arbitrary length. Both models trade autoregressive generation for a
complexity that grows with the number of sampling steps. Text-conditioned
environmental sounds are generated by AudioGen \cite{audiogen}. All of these
models generate a fixed-length segment of audio where the end of the
generated audio is not connected to the start.

LoopGen \cite{loopgen} creates a generative model that can be used to
generate loops without retraining. LoopGen adapts MAGNeT, a masked
non-autoregressive generative model, to generate its own token sequence in a
loop. The end of the sequence is generated with the context of the beginning
of the sequence, and the join between the end and the beginning is generated
rather than later stitched on. While LoopGen leverages the loopable
generation from MAGNeT, the loopable generation does not easily translate to
autoregressive models. Instead, a loop can be cut after generation at a
structural position identified by self-supervised multi-level
representations \cite{buisson} or barwise self-similarity \cite{marmoret}.
AffectMachinePop \cite{affectmachine} is an expert system which can generate
pop music in real time from a desired valence--arousal trajectory
\cite{kang} without the use of a GPU. This allows for direct manipulation of
the perceived affect in the generated music. However, the desired affect
must be provided externally.

There have been three relatively recent approaches that create from user
context. MetaBGM \cite{metabgm} uses an LLM to convert game and interactive
actions into musical descriptions which are then converted into music by a
text-to-music system. The music is maintained across scene changes, but the
full state is sent to a cloud LLM on each request. Discussion Jockey~2
\cite{suzawa} transcribes speech from an online meeting and then converts it
to prompts for MusicGen following the discussion. A user study found a
relaxation effect but the full transcript is sent. Context-AI Tunes
\cite{contextai} uses a combination of a user reported stress level and a
picture of their current context to create a prompt for relaxation music. The
system was tested on 26 users but has active input and the image exits the
device. Casper \cite{casper} is a browser extension that cleanses user input
prior to sending it to a cloud LLM. It uses a local cascade of identifier
rules, NER for locations and names and then an LLM to identify sensitive
information. Casper cleanses rather than creates and has no exposure rate at
the corpus level.

FrugalGPT \cite{frugalgpt} created the confidence-gated cascade. Queries are
attempted using increasingly cheaper LLMs and a learned scorer makes a
decision to either use the response or proceed to the next LLM. It was shown
that performance comparable to GPT-4 can be achieved with up to 98\% less
inference cost. Hybrid LLM \cite{hybridllm} attempts each query with the LLM
predicted to be most appropriate for the query based on difficulty. Up to
40\% fewer large models are used while maintaining output quality.
Teacher-student caching \cite{cachemeifyoucan} uses an online small model
trained on outputs from an LLM to reduce the number of times the LLM is
queried. Agreement-based cascading \cite{agreement} only progresses to the
next model when a group of low cost models do not agree. These systems focus
on reducing cost and latency rather than the content seen by the expensive
model. WeInfer \cite{weinfer} demonstrates the viability of running an LLM
within the browser using WebGPU.

\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Gaps}

Prior work leaves three major research gaps. First, system-level properties
have gone unmeasured: evaluation ends at a system's output, typically audio
quality. Second, cascades have been optimized for cost efficiency rather than
disclosure; no existing cascade permits adjustable exposure. Third, the
paucity of generators that produce loops and the missing seams at
post-processing have gone unmeasured. Web2Music seeks to close these gaps by
systematically deriving music description from any web page passively and
attacking all three at once: it enables the measurement of the entire
pipeline from the DOM to audio, treating exposure as an adjustable rate, and
ensuring all loops are closed with a measurable seam.

\subsection{Problem statement}

Web2Music is judged on four properties. \textbf{Latency:} how soon after a mood
is decided something is audible, and how soon page-level audio follows.
\textbf{Disclosure:} how much page text leaves the device; this is measured, not
assumed. \textbf{Continuity:} clips loop without gaps. \textbf{Restraint:} pages
with sensitive content produce no audio and send nothing. The zero-shot and LLM
providers, and the key proxy in front of them, are designed to receive only the
page title, a two-sentence description and the top keywords. The generation
service is designed to receive only the music profile (category, mood, tempo and
related fields); with a local GPU that channel disappears too. A network audit
(Section~\ref{sec:res-restraint}) checks both statements.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 \cite{mv3} Chrome extension plus one HTTP
generation service. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} follows one page from DOM to speaker
in nine steps and marks the two persistent stores: an IndexedDB vector store
on the device and a Postgres cache index behind the generation service.

\subsection{Page descriptor}
\label{sec:arch-descriptor}

One function assembles the descriptor from four signals: text; a
384-dimensional MiniLM sentence embedding computed in-browser; colour, an
area-weighted HSL histogram over up to 800 elements; and behaviour, scroll and
cursor velocity; plus a readability score. The embedding is matched by cosine
similarity against an IndexedDB store of prior pages to flag revisits, capped
at 2,000 entries.

\subsection{Classifier cascade}
\label{sec:arch-cascade}

The classifier maps a descriptor to one of 13 content categories and one of 11
moods, and these select the music profile and the prompt. The shipped default is
the full three-stage cascade with the on-device middle stage (configuration A6b in
Section~\ref{sec:res-classification}). \textbf{Keyword
stage:} at the keyword level, the keyword map is used to find the category,
while moods take the keyword score, colour, and behaviour biases together into
account \cite{jonauskaite}. The cascade also stops at this stage, at zero
cost, when confidence is $\geq 0.5$. \textbf{Zero-shot stage:} the text is
scored against thirteen category hypotheses using natural-language
inference (NLI) \cite{laurer}. With $s_{(1)}$ and $s_{(2)}$ the highest and
second-highest scores, the stage takes the top label only if
\begin{equation}
s_{(1)} \ge \tau \quad\text{and}\quad s_{(1)} - s_{(2)} \ge \delta ,
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
that is, the best label is strong enough and clearly ahead of the runner-up; in
any other case it abstains and the page moves to the next stage
\cite{agreement}. The defaults are $\tau = 0.45$ and $\delta = 0.10$. The stage
has two interchangeable back ends: an online hosted bart-large-mnli endpoint, and a
quantised ONNX nli-deberta-v3-xsmall model that runs in the offscreen
document without sending any data. In the ablations the hosted back end is replaced by
the same bart-large-mnli weights run locally at full precision (fp32 ONNX in
transformers.js, no data leaves the device); the 8-bit quantised export of that model
returned near-uniform scores and was not used, and the deployed offscreen model remains the
quantised deberta-v3-xsmall. \textbf{LLM stage:} the gpt-oss-20b model
running on Groq is used, with the temperature set to 0. A second, fully local configuration
uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (4-bit, served by Ollama) in the same role; it builds the page
profiles for the fidelity study and is reported separately from the Groq results. An eight-second
timeout limit is implemented; in case of timeout, the decision would be made
on the basis of keywords. The chosen mood will stay the same for five seconds
in the confidence period before the musical transition to avoid the
perceivable fluctuations due to user scrolling. The externally hosted stages
are reached through a proxy that holds the API keys, so no credential is
embedded in the extension. The share of pages that leave the device is then
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} P(\text{page } i \text{ reaches an off-device stage}),
\label{eq:disclosure}
\end{equation}
which is the quantity the gate $(\tau,\delta)$ adjusts. Every stage's output is
logged, and the LLM's raw answers are cached, so any configuration can be
replayed offline without querying the model again
(\S\ref{sec:res-classification}).

\textbf{Sensitive-page check.} Before either hosted stage, a term list flags
sensitive pages. An optional entailment check then scores the page against three
hypotheses: a personal crisis, an academic or news discussion of a difficult
topic, and everyday content with no difficult topic. The third hypothesis is what
lets an ordinary page (a recipe, a tutorial) score as neither crisis nor
discussion. A sensitive page produces no audio and is never sent to the LLM.

\textbf{From mood to prompt.} A committed mood picks a particular row from a
per-mood table, including parameters like tempo span, keys, timbre,
instruments, reverb, and style. Tempo is calculated using interpolation
according to the energy parameter in the chosen range and then tuned with the
help of one of nine page-type tuners and seven time-of-day tuners. Key is
determined by valence, while darker pages are given more reverb. This profile
fills the prompt template ending with loop-based directives (without vocals,
without percussion on the loop point).

\subsection{Generation, loop synthesis and caching}
\label{sec:arch-gen}

The service verifies the query and queries the Postgres cache index (steps
5--7), where the index is exact on the mood, style, and key but also on the
energy, valence, arousal, tempo, and duration buckets. If the match was
successful, the URL and loop point are returned. In case of a miss,
MusicGen-small (300 million parameters, default sampling) is run on one
batching worker. The back end is a start-up setting: MusicGen-small (default),
MusicGen-medium, or AudioLDM2-music, a latent-diffusion model, so that
Section~\ref{sec:res-generators} can compare generators under one pipeline. The worker batches up to four queries within a 150\,ms time
window, and it generates 50 tokens per second of requested audio that is then
processed into an audio clip with index upserting. In case of failure during
any step, the system uses a pre-generated audio clip for the particular mood.

\textbf{Loop synthesis.} Chroma descriptors give a self-similarity curve
\cite{marmoret} whose peaks, together with the bar boundaries, are the
candidate loop points (at least 3\,s into the clip). The default selector cuts
at each candidate, applies an equal-power crossfade (250\,ms in the shipped
build), and keeps the candidate whose crossfaded seam is smallest:
\begin{equation}
J = |\Delta E| + \lambda\,|\Delta c| ,
\label{eq:seam}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta E$ is the energy jump in dB between the end and start of the
loop, $\Delta c$ the jump in spectral centroid in Hz, and $\lambda = 1/5000$ per
Hz, so the centroid term only breaks ties. The earlier rule, which cuts at the
chroma-similarity maximum, remains available as a switch for comparison
(\S\ref{sec:res-loop}). Loudness is normalised to $-18$\,LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) \cite{ebu128} and
the clip is exported as Ogg/Opus, which, unlike MP3, adds no padding at every
repeat.

\subsection{Playback and restraint}
\label{sec:arch-playback}

Playback is embedded inside an off-screen document. Looping is performed by
two decks using the raw audio buffer inputs alternatively and then applying
three gain stages (user volume control, tab reduction, and mood fade), where
all the gain stages multiply and not overwrite each other. The
fallback-then-swap strategy involves accessing the fallback clip based on
mood and the next generation simultaneously, playing back the fallback clip
and fading in the new generation based on the equal power curve of the seam
for 3 seconds; the previous generation is killed when there is a new mood.
\textbf{Restraint:} sensitively flagged pages do not have any audio playbacks
and are skipped before the start of either of the two hosted stages. When the
current tab is generating audio, then the player is muted. On the other hand,
background tabs playing audio decrease the gain to 0.1 of the player's
volume. URLs of payment sites, banking websites, and internal browser URLs are
exempted from the process. The system keeps up to 2,000 telemetry events in a local ring buffer, with each
URL replaced by an HMAC-SHA-256 digest under a random per-install key (a plain
hash of a URL can be reversed by hashing likely URLs). The buffer is never sent
anywhere unless the user exports it. What leaves the device is: in the fully local
configuration (A6), only the music profile sent to the generation service; in the
default configuration (A6b), additionally the title, description and keywords of
pages that the on-device stages could not decide, sent to the LLM through the
proxy.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:res-design}

We report a systems benchmark, a cascade ablation with a held-out test set, a
browsing simulation of how often generated audio is heard, objective
conditioning-fidelity measures, a loop quality study, and a restraint audit.
Percentiles are nearest-rank. Where an interval is given it is a 95\%
percentile bootstrap over pages or clips (10,000 resamples).

\textbf{Corpora.} The \emph{development set} is 255 real pages in seven slices
(general, vocabulary mismatch, non-English, mixed-genre, sensitive, near-miss,
bypass), 252 of them category-labelled. Every threshold is chosen on it. The
\emph{test set} is \tbd{S-E: n} further pages captured after the system was
frozen, from a URL list fixed before any classifier saw them, and is scored once
with the thresholds chosen on development. No URL was suggested by an LLM; the
source mix is \tbd{S-E: source mix}, and \tbd{S-E: n post-cutoff} pages were
published after the LLM's knowledge cutoff, so that contamination can be checked.
The \emph{adversarial slice} is 47 hand-written pages (29 sensitive, 18 not)
built from the detector's own term lists; it is used only to choose the
sensitivity gates. The \emph{black-box set} is \tbd{S-F: n} real crisis and
support pages found by searching for support resources, not by using the term
lists.

\textbf{Labels.} Each page carries the category it was collected for. Three of
the authors labelled every page independently. For the test set the labelling was
blind: the collection category, the system's predictions and the other labellers'
answers were hidden, page order was shuffled per labeller, and the guidelines
were frozen after a calibration round on development pages and before any test
page was labelled. The labellers are the authors, not independent annotators; we
state this as a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The gold label is the
majority of the three, and \tbd{S-E: n ties} ties were settled by discussion. On
the development set the majority resolves for 208 pages and agrees with the
collection category on 85\%. Only category and binary sensitivity are labelled;
mood is not labelled or evaluated. No LLM chose pages, labelled them or judged
any output; the LLM stage (model, prompt, temperature 0) was frozen before the
test set was opened and its raw answers are cached, so every replay uses the same
answers.

store, 130 generated and 11 per-mood fallbacks. Table~\ref{tab:hardware} lists the
three hardware tiers. Generation time and the browsing simulation are reported

\subsection{Annotation agreement}
\label{sec:res-agreement}

Over 255 pages, Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{kalpha}, a chance-corrected
agreement coefficient, is 0.635 for category,
0.419 for binary sensitivity and 0.195 for mood. Category agreement
approaches but does not reach the 0.667 reliability threshold, so we score
the cascade on category and read its results as tentative. Mood agreement
stays low even after coarsening to valence--arousal quadrants \cite{kang}
($\alpha = 0.121$) or NRC VAD norms \cite{nrcvad} ($\alpha = 0.300$): the
mood of a page is a matter of taste. Web2Music therefore treats mood as a
stylistic mapping from page signals rather than a classification target, and mood
is not evaluated anywhere in this paper. On the test set, labelled blind under
frozen guidelines, $\alpha$ is \tbd{S-E: test alpha category} for category and
\tbd{S-E: test alpha sensitivity} for binary sensitivity, with per-category values
in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Descriptor cost and signal value}
\label{sec:res-descriptor}

On 105 live websites loaded in an automated Chromium browser, descriptor
construction takes a median of 86\,ms against a 1,989\,ms navigation, and
boilerplate removal keeps 80.8\% of visible text. Embedding inference is
stubbed in this benchmark, so 86\,ms is the extraction cost excluding the model;
with the real MiniLM embedding model, loaded once and warm as in the extension, on 166 sites

\subsection{Classification and disclosure}
\label{sec:res-classification}

Table~\ref{tab:cascade} and Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} give the result. The
single-stage baselines bracket the design space: keyword-only (A1) discloses
nothing at .190 accuracy, and LLM-only (A3) reaches .694 by disclosing every
page. Keyword-to-LLM (A4) comes within .005 of A3's macro-F1 (paired 95\% CI
$[-.041, +.033]$) while keeping 24\% of pages on the device. Every macro-F1
interval is about $\pm.05$ wide, so smaller differences are not resolved by
this corpus.

A local middle stage is what makes the cascade a privacy mechanism. With the
on-device zero-shot model, A6b matches A4's macro-F1 (.689; paired difference
.000, 95\% CI $[-.044, +.042]$) while the fraction of pages leaving the device
falls from .757 to .467, a 38\% reduction. The local model answers in a
median of 390\,ms against 1.74\,s for the hosted one.

The local gate is the disclosure dial. Replaying A6b with the local stage's
gate moved traces the green frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}. Lowering
the score threshold from 0.45 to 0.30 cuts off-device from .467 to .290 at
macro-F1 .675, and opening the gate entirely keeps every page on the device
at .594, level with the hosted zero-shot model alone (A2, .595); A6 at the
default gate reaches only .500 because declined pages fall to a default
category. On the development set
these gate cells locate the trade-off. For the held-out test set, the gates are
chosen on development pages only and every configuration is then scored once
(Table~\ref{tab:cascade-test}): A6b reaches macro-F1 \tbd{S-E: A6b test F1} against
\tbd{S-E: A4 test F1} for keyword-to-LLM, a paired difference of \tbd{S-E: paired
diff}, with off-device rates of \tbd{S-E: A6b off-device} and \tbd{S-E: A4
off-device}. Splitting accuracy by the stage that decided shows how much of each
score comes from the LLM: \tbd{S-E: by-stage}. On the \tbd{S-E: n post-cutoff}
test pages published after the LLM's knowledge cutoff, macro-F1 is \tbd{S-E:
post-cutoff F1} against \tbd{S-E: pre-cutoff F1} on the rest.

A hosted middle stage only redistributes disclosure. A5 cuts LLM-specific
exposure from .757 to .220, but every page it keeps from the LLM reaches the
hosted zero-shot endpoint instead, so total off-device stays at .757, as the
cascade's structure dictates. A twelve-cell sweep of the hosted gate shows
it: macro-F1 stays within .658--.685 and LLM exposure moves over a
$5.4\times$ range (.106--.576), while total off-device holds at .756--.757 in
every cell. The hosted gate chooses which provider sees a page; only a local
backend changes whether one does.

\subsection{Generation latency and fallback-then-swap}
\label{sec:res-latency}

Table~\ref{tab:latency} and Fig.~\ref{fig:coldgen} show the routes a request
can take. The fallback clip is served in 7\,ms, so the audible start is never
delayed by the generation process. On the Apple M4, all 140 cold generations
of 28 seconds complete, with a median of 72 seconds and a real-time factor (RTF, generation seconds per second of audio) of
1.8--2.3 across durations; the generation process itself takes up 71.5
seconds of that median, and every other stage takes under a second. With the GPU disabled, the same machine generates a 10\,s clip in a median of 38.5\,s
and a 15\,s clip in 62.2\,s (real-time factor 3.9--4.2; 12 requests per length spread
over three moods, none timed out), so the fallback clip plays for a minute or more
while a cold clip is generated. Cache hits on the CPU laptop took 2.09 seconds, almost all of it
spent in the cache lookup, and using pooled Postgres connections lowers that
lookup to 8\,ms. On the RTX 5060 a cold 15\,s clip takes a median of
13.3\,s and a 28\,s clip 24.3\,s
(real-time factor 0.87--0.91 end to end across 8--28\,s clips, of which the generator call alone is 0.83--0.85; 33 cold requests per length, spread over all 11 moods, none failed or fell back), measured end to end with the real
classifier, embedding model and generator over 110 page loads per
setup (11 fixture pages $\times$ 10 repeats, of which 10 loads per setup generate and the rest hit the cache), so the figures in the abstract no longer rest on stubbed components.
Concurrent requests do not raise throughput: $k = 2$, 4 and 8 simultaneous 15\,s requests finish in 28.3, 54.8 and 111.3\,s, within 1--2\% of running the same requests one after another (speedup 1.02, 1.01 and 1.01$\times$; five repeats per level, rotating moods, none failed), so the worker's batching brings no gain on this GPU and the queueing delay grows linearly with $k$.
On the CPU-only configuration a cold 15\,s clip takes about a minute, so generated audio
arrives only in longer visits, and for most visits the system behaves as a mood-level
player with cache hits (Section~\ref{sec:res-exposure}).

An automated browser tests the built extension against eleven fixture pages
with generation and classification stubbed out: out of 55 page loads, 54
became audible, with a median latency of 6.86 seconds from navigation. The
first extraction finishes in 0.86 seconds, and the mood then stays stable
throughout the confidence window (median of 6.0 seconds); once the mood
commits, the fallback becomes audible in a median of 36 milliseconds (p95: 54
milliseconds), regardless of what the generator is doing. This is the latency
property of \S\ref{sec:gaps}: the remaining latency is the confidence window,
which is a design parameter, not model latency. The p95 value (11.28 seconds)
is due to one fixture requiring five extraction passes before its mood
commits.

\subsection{How often generated audio is heard}
\label{sec:res-exposure}

The 36\,ms figure describes the mood-level clip (L1), not generated audio. Whether
a listener ever reaches the page-level clip (L3) depends on how long they stay
compared with how long generation takes:
\begin{equation}
P(\mathrm{L3}) = P\bigl(T_{\mathrm{gen}} + W < T_{\mathrm{dwell}}\bigr),
\label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{gen}}$ is the generation time, $W = 5$\,s is the confidence
window before a mood is committed, and $T_{\mathrm{dwell}}$ is the time on the
page. We estimate it by simulating browsing sessions. Moods follow a Markov chain
fitted to the corpus mood frequencies; dwell times are Weibull-distributed
\cite{liudwell} with the median swept from 10 to 120\,s and a longer median for
long-form pages; generation times are drawn from the measured distributions of
each tier and clip length, with timeouts treated as censored; there is one
worker, generation is cancelled when the next page needs a different clip, and the
99-entry pre-warm grid and L3 a fresh generation that finishes before the visit ends. Cache entries are keyed on
the exact profile (mood, tempo, key, energy and the style string), and the cache starts empty
for each simulated session. The pre-warm grid is credited only with the hit rate measured on
real profiles: replaying the profile builder's keys for the 235 corpus pages, none falls on a
grid key (75 distinct keys occur in all), so L2 arises only from an earlier visit to the same
key within a session. Mood frequencies are those of the corpus (curious 36\%, focused 18\%,
neutral 12\%, calm 2\%).

The simulation says how often generated audio \emph{could} be heard under stated
assumptions; it is not a measurement of listeners. On the GPU, page-level audio is
heard on 52\% of visits and accounts for 75\% of listening time at a 15\,s clip; on the M4 the figures are 48\% and 73\%, and on the CPU 34\% and 64\%, so generator speed matters less than dwell time and the confidence window. The share rises with dwell time (GPU, 15\,s: 37\%, 52\% and 64\% of visits at median dwell 10, 30 and 120\,s) and is higher on long-form pages (52\%, 62\% and 68\%). Two bounds frame these figures. If half of the pages did hit the pre-warm grid, which we do not observe, the GPU share would fall to 29\% of visits at 30\,s, because a grid hit is served as L2. If the cache persisted across sessions, then since the corpus has only 75 distinct keys nearly every committed visit would be served from the cache after the first sessions (81\% of visits are L2 at 30\,s); this is a trivial upper bound on cache use, not an estimate of it. The M4 row rests on the three-request-per-length duration sweep.

\subsection{Conditioning fidelity}
\label{sec:res-fidelity}

With no listening study, we ask a narrower, objective question: does audio
generated for a page agree with the mood the system intended for it, more than
audio generated for a page of a different mood does? We generate
702 clips (234 pages $\times$ 3 seeds) on the GPU and
compare each with a \emph{shuffled} control, a clip from a page whose mood is at
least two steps away in valence--arousal space, plus a page-agnostic fixed-prompt
control. Four non-generative scorers are used: Essentia valence/arousal regressors
and the MTG-Jamendo mood tagger; LAION-CLAP, which retrieves a page's own clip
among 10 candidates from the page's title and lead text (chance is 10\%); and
Audiobox Aesthetics for quality. No LLM or audio-language model judges any output.

\subsection{Loop quality}
\label{sec:res-loop}

Of the 141 delivered clips, all fall within their duration bounds, and
loudness lands at $-18.09$\,LUFS against the $-18.0$ target. The 50\,ms
crossfade reduces the median seam energy discontinuity from 3.73 to 2.64\,dB
and improves 58.9\% of seams. Delivered loops keep a median 50.3\% of the
requested duration; since the loop repeats gaplessly, this sets the loop
period rather than the playback length, so a 28\,s request repeats about
every 14\,s.

Table~\ref{tab:seam} and Fig.~\ref{fig:seam} compare the two levers on the
same 113 clips. Increasing the crossfade tenfold, from 50 to 500\,ms, lowers
the median energy jump by only 25\%, and 67\% of seams remain above 1\,dB.
The measured-seam selector, which keeps the 50\,ms crossfade and chooses
among the detector's own candidate points, brings the share of seams above
1\,dB down to 12\% and above 3\,dB to 1\% (from 74\% and 36\%), and the
median drops from 2.44 to 0.14\,dB. It makes the seam better on 102 of 113
clips, worse on two, and preserves internal duration retention (.695 vs.\
.690). The selector minimises this same energy jump together with a small
spectral-centroid term, so these numbers show how far the candidates let the
jump fall, not that listeners hear it; the 500\,ms crossfade still gives the
smallest centroid jump. The shipped build uses a 250\,ms crossfade with the
measured-seam selector; this table compares the levers on the 50\,ms setting they
were measured at.

Two further checks replace listener judgement. First, a fresh study of 220 clips
(11 moods $\times$ 20 fixed seeds) compares the two rules on the shipped crossfade.
Of the 220, 176 are scoreable: 39 have a tail shorter than 500\,ms, too short for the
250\,ms crossfade, and 5 have no detectable loop point. On these 176 the median
$|\Delta E|$ is 2.08\,dB [1.69, 2.33] for chroma similarity and 0.16\,dB [0.12, 0.23]
for the measured seam, and the share above 1\,dB falls from 72.7\% [65.9, 79.0] to
8.5\% [4.5, 12.5] (McNemar $p = 2\times10^{-34}$; the 145 clips that fit the full
crossfade sweep give 71.7\% and 6.2\%). Second, the loops are \emph{gapless}: rendering each clip through the
browser's own decoder and a looping buffer source for 10 cycles, 30 of 30
clips show no inserted silence and are sample-identical from cycle to cycle, so the
Ogg/Opus container adds no padding at repeats. Relative to each clip's own
variation, the seam's spectral-centroid change lies within the clip's 95th percentile of interior
change in 91.4\% [87.7, 95.0] of the 220 clips. The energy jump is within its 95th
percentile in all 220, which is expected because the selector minimises that jump, so we
do not count it as evidence. Gapless playback is shown; a seam being
\emph{inaudible} is not claimed.

\subsection{Restraint}
\label{sec:res-restraint}

All fourteen executable restraint policies pass their tests: silence on
sensitive pages \cite{psyguard}, muting and ducking around existing audio,
bypass of payment and browser-internal pages, hashed-URL telemetry, and seven
unit cases. The adversarial slice then attacks the sensitivity predicate.

The term lists are precise, producing only three false positives, all
near-miss academic pages such as a grief-counselling degree. However, a
white-box slice built from the lists' own vocabulary evades them on 19 of 29
sensitive pages, through out-of-list terms (7), euphemisms (4), non-English
text (4), and single ambiguous terms (4). Re-scoring with the entailment
model detects all 29, including the four non-English pages, with scores
between 0.83 and 0.92 (Table~\ref{tab:sensitive}). This run used the hosted
bart-large-mnli backend, which receives every page without a hard-severe
term; in this configuration, the check itself therefore discloses the pages
it is meant to protect. The disclosure property holds only with the on-device
backend. The hosted two-hypothesis check also overtriggers: its eleven false
positives include all six plainly benign pages, such as a Rust tutorial, because
the prompt offers only crisis or reference discussion and no ``unrelated'' option.
The shipped check therefore adds that third hypothesis and runs on-device. With
its gates chosen on the hand-written slice only, it detects \tbd{S-F: recall
[Wilson CI]} of the \tbd{S-F: n} real black-box pages (\tbd{S-F: non-English
recall} of the non-English ones) and raises a false alarm on \tbd{S-F: FPR
[Wilson CI]} of \tbd{S-F: n benign} benign pages, with a median latency of
\tbd{S-F: ms}\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:sensitive}). All detector claims come from the
black-box and benign sets, not from the hand-written slice.

\textbf{Network audit.} To check the disclosure claims on the wire, we recorded every
request made by the service worker, the offscreen document, the content scripts
and the pages while the built extension browsed the corpus. In the fully local
configuration (A6, the LLM stage switched off), no request body contained page text
(12 fixture pages $\times$ 3 visits; the only traffic was the generation service, 850 bytes per
page). In the default configuration (A6b), page text left the device on 30 of the 36 visits (83\%),
all to the LLM proxy and nowhere else (5.9\,kB per page to the proxy and 0.8\,kB to the generation
service). The fixtures were written to exercise the cascade and are not representative of browsing,
so this rate is not the disclosure rate (47\% of the 255 development pages in the replayed cascade); what the
audit establishes is the destination of the text. No telemetry left the browser, and every request to the
generation service carried the music profile and nothing else. The generation service therefore sees
the category and mood of each page; with a local GPU it is local too.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music plays background music conditioned on the page being read. Each
pipeline step is assessed against the properties of Section~\ref{sec:gaps}.
Running the middle classifier stage on the device keeps the keyword-to-LLM
macro-F1 on the development set and cuts off-device page transfers by 38\%;
the gate sets how much further disclosure is reduced, and the held-out result is
in Table~\ref{tab:cascade-test}. A mood-level clip is audible 36\,ms after the
mood decision whatever the generation time, and the share of visits in which the
page-level clip is also heard is estimated in Section~\ref{sec:res-exposure}.
Choosing the loop point by measured seam removes most large energy jumps at the
loop, and the loops are gapless.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{No perceptual evaluation.} We did not run a listening study, so we make
no claim that listeners find the music fits the page, that the loops sound
seamless, or that the 14\,s loop period does not cause fatigue. The protocol and
instruments for a within-subjects study (page-matched against shuffled audio;
forced choice, Likert ratings, dwell, skip and mute) are released with the code.
The conditioning-fidelity measures of Section~\ref{sec:res-fidelity} are proxies
and inherit the biases of the models behind them: CLAP is trained on music
captions, not web pages, and the mood-to-tag mapping is an assumption.

\textbf{Author annotation.} The three labellers are authors. Labelling was blind
and the guidelines were frozen first, but this is not independent annotation, and
agreement between authors who share a design may overstate reliability.

\textbf{Simulation assumptions.} How often generated audio is heard depends on
dwell times that we model rather than measure: the Weibull shape and the long-form
multiplier. The share of pages whose profile is in the pre-warm grid is measured on the
corpus (0 of 235), but the corpus is not a sample of real browsing. The sweep over the median dwell shows the sensitivity, not the
truth for any particular user.

\textbf{Other limits.} The held-out test set is small, so macro-F1 intervals are
about $\pm .05$ wide and smaller differences are not resolved. The LLM stage in the main
configuration is a hosted model; its answers are cached for replay but its behaviour can
change under the same model name, and the local Qwen2.5-7B configuration gives different
answers and is not interchangeable with it. The hardware is one machine with one GPU, plus two
CPU-class tiers. Future work includes conditioning the generator to produce
loop-closed content, independent annotators and a listening study, and applying the
same pipeline to other continuous contexts such as documents and code editors.
