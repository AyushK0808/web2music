\begin{abstract}
Generative audio models can produce plausible instrumental music from a text
prompt, but placing one inside an always-on consumer application raises three
problems that model quality alone does not solve: the prompt has to be derived
from something, the derivation must not leak the user's browsing history, and
the model is far too slow to sit on the critical path of a page load. We
present Web2Music, a Chrome extension that generates mood-adaptive ambient
music conditioned on the page the user is reading. Three contributions follow
from those constraints. First, a four-signal extraction layer, comprising
article text, a local sentence embedding, dominant page colour, and
scroll and cursor behaviour, produces a compact page descriptor at a median
cost of 86 ms per page in a real renderer across 105 websites, excluding the
embedding stage, which was stubbed for this measurement; an ablation finds
that two of the four signals move nothing measurable and a third, colour,
moves little. Second, a
three-tier classifier, consisting of a free keyword heuristic, a zero-shot
natural-language-inference tier that may abstain, and a remote large language
model as a last resort, maps that descriptor onto an eleven-way mood and a
thirteen-way content category; because the middle tier abstains rather than
guesses, its two confidence thresholds form a continuous dial between accuracy
and the fraction of pages whose text reaches the generative remote model,
though not, with the hosted zero-shot backend measured here, over how much
text leaves the device in total. Third, a
fallback-then-swap playback design hides a generator whose median cold
generation takes 139 s behind a pre-generated per-mood clip served in 7 ms,
while bar-snapped loop-point detection and a 250 ms equal-power crossfade turn
a one-shot generator's output into loopable material. We report a systems
evaluation over 105 websites, 144 generation requests and 141 generated clips,
an adversarial audit of the system's refusal behaviour, and a cost model
showing the cascade classifies a thousand pages for 0.0132 USD. We also report
four negative results: the crossfade removes a median of only 0.93 dB of seam
energy discontinuity, the delivered clip retains a median of 50.3 percent of
its requested duration, the sensitive-content detector misses 65.5 percent
of an adversarial crisis slice, and three independent annotators agree on the
system's eleven-way mood label at only $\alpha=0.195$. Collapsing that label
onto Russell's valence-arousal quadrants moves agreement in opposite
directions depending on the mapping used, down to $\alpha=0.121$ under this
system's own encoding and up to $\alpha=0.300$ under published NRC-VAD word
norms -- both still short of a usable threshold, and evidence that 
mood is underdetermined by page content at both the resolution the system
reports and the coarser one a circumplex model offers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Background music is one of the few media people consume while doing something
else. It is chosen once, for a session, and then ignored: a lo-fi playlist runs
for four hours across a tax form, a horror wiki and a birthday message without
ever acknowledging that anything changed. The music is context-free because,
historically, making it context-aware required either a human curator or a
library large enough to cover every context in advance.

Text-conditioned generative audio models remove that constraint in principle.
MusicGen \cite{musicgen} generates instrumental audio from a natural-language
description in a single autoregressive pass over EnCodec \cite{encodec} tokens;
MusicLM \cite{musiclm} does so hierarchically at higher fidelity; AudioGen
\cite{audiogen} and AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} extend the same idea to general
audio. If the description can be derived automatically from what the user is
doing, the music can track it, and the curator is no longer needed.

Deriving it is where the engineering lives. This paper concerns what happens
when a generative audio model is placed behind a consumer browser extension and
made to respond to the open web. Three constraints dominate, and none of them
is about audio quality.

\textbf{The prompt has to come from somewhere.} A web page is not a prompt. It
is a DOM containing navigation chrome, advertisements, a colour scheme, and a
reader whose scroll velocity says something about how they are engaging with
it. Turning that into ``lo-fi study, minimal electronic, deep focus, 88 BPM,
D minor'' is a multi-stage inference problem over noisy, adversarially
formatted input.

\textbf{Classification is a privacy decision.} The most accurate way to
classify a page is to send its text to a large language model. Doing that on
every page turns a music player into a browsing-history exfiltration channel.
The interesting design question is not whether to use an LLM, but how few pages
can be sent to one.

\textbf{The model is too slow to be on the critical path.} We measure a median
of 139 s ($n=3$ live generations; Section \ref{sec:res-latency}) to generate
a 28 s clip on a CPU laptop, against a page load of about
two seconds. No amount of optimisation closes a two-order-of-magnitude gap; the
interaction has to be designed so that the gap is not visible.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item A four-signal page descriptor and its measured cost, characterised over
105 real websites in an instrumented Chromium with per-stage latency and forced
layout counts. An ablation against the full corpus, run offline under the
keyword and fixed-rule tiers rather than the live cascade (Section
\ref{sec:res-signal-ablation}), finds category is decided
by text alone regardless of what else is present, behaviour and the
embedding move mood on zero pages, and colour moves it weakly, on 7.5 percent
of pages: two of the four signals earn nothing measurable, and a third earns
little.

\item An abstaining three-tier classifier that functions as a privacy dial: a
zero-shot NLI tier between a free keyword heuristic and a remote LLM declines
when its top score, or its margin over the runner-up, falls below a
threshold. Its two thresholds trade accuracy against \emph{LLM-specific
exposure}, the fraction of pages whose text reaches the generative remote
tier; with the hosted zero-shot backend measured here, that dial redistributes
disclosure between providers rather than reducing total exposure.

\item A latency-hiding playback design, measured end to end on the server side:
instant fallback followed by an abort-on-stale crossfaded swap, reported with
its full latency distribution including failure modes.

\item Loop synthesis from a non-loop model: bar-snapped chroma self-similarity,
a tuned equal-power crossfade, and a gapless Ogg/Opus container, evaluated
objectively over 141 generated clips.

\item An evaluation apparatus that constrains overclaiming. Every number is
emitted by a named script against a machine-readable metric registry that
rejects any figure consuming an undeclared metric, and reports artefacts whose
input data does not exist as blocked rather than building them from
placeholders.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper reviews related work (\S\ref{sec:related}), states
the research gaps and problem statement (\S\ref{sec:gaps}), describes the
system architecture (\S\ref{sec:arch}), presents results
(\S\ref{sec:results}), and closes with conclusion, limitations and future
scope (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}--\S\ref{sec:future}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Text-conditioned music and audio generation}

MusicGen \cite{musicgen} is the generator used in this work. It models discrete
EnCodec \cite{encodec} audio tokens with a single-stage transformer, avoiding
the cascade of models earlier systems required, and conditions on a free-text
description, which is what makes an automatically derived prompt possible at
all. Jukebox \cite{jukebox} established that raw-audio generative modelling of
music was feasible but at a computational cost far beyond interactive use.
MusicLM \cite{musiclm} produces higher-fidelity long-form audio from text via a
hierarchical sequence-to-sequence formulation, and latent-diffusion approaches
such as AudioLDM \cite{audioldm} and timing-conditioned latent diffusion
\cite{stableaudio} have since improved both quality and speed. AudioGen
\cite{audiogen} applies the same conditioning idea to environmental audio.
AudioCraft \cite{audiocraft} provides the reference implementation for MusicGen
and EnCodec. More recently, SongGen \cite{songgen} extends the same
single-stage autoregressive approach to full song generation with lyrics and
vocals produced in one pass, evidence that this model family is still the
active frontier rather than a closed 2023 snapshot. We use the smallest
MusicGen variant because it is the only one in the family that produces a
28 s clip on a consumer CPU in a time that a fallback path can plausibly
hide.

\subsection{Affective and context-aware music selection}

A separate line of work drives music from an explicit affective specification
rather than free text. AffectMachine-Pop \cite{affectmachine} is a controllable
expert system that generates pop music in real time from a target
valence--arousal point, demonstrating that real-time affective control is
achievable when the generator is rule-based rather than learned. Web2Music
occupies the opposite corner of that trade-off: a learned generator with
greater timbral variety, and an interaction design that pays for its latency.
MusicSense \cite{musicsense} recommends existing tracks from an emotional model
of the text a user is reading and is the closest predecessor in intent; the
difference is that it retrieves from a library where we generate, a choice we
quantify rather than assert in Section \ref{sec:res-retrieval}.

The affective vocabulary itself derives from dimensional models of emotion,
principally Russell's circumplex \cite{russell} and Thayer's arousal model
\cite{thayer}, which we use to place moods in a valence--arousal space and to
map colour and behaviour signals onto shifts within it. Music emotion
recognition, surveyed by Kim et al. \cite{kim2010} and Yang and Chen
\cite{yang2012}, addresses the inverse problem of inferring the mood
\emph{of} a piece of music, using audio and lyrics jointly
\cite{multimodalmood}; the relationship between mood metadata and other
descriptors was characterised early by Hu and Downie \cite{hu2007}. Our
problem is the reverse, and is not strictly a classification problem with a
ground truth, a point we return to in Section \ref{sec:limitations}.

Context-aware computing supplies the framing for a system that reacts to
implicit signals rather than explicit commands \cite{schilit, dey}, and calm
technology \cite{weiser} supplies the design stance: an ambient system's most
important property is often its ability to remain in the periphery. The
psychological literature on background music and cognitive performance is
mixed; a meta-analysis by K\"ampfe et al. \cite{kampfe} finds small and
context-dependent effects, and the irrelevant-speech effect \cite{salame}
suggests why instrumental, non-lyrical material is the safer choice for a
system that plays while people read.

\subsection{Zero-shot classification and model cascades}

The middle tier of our classifier follows the natural-language-inference
formulation of zero-shot classification: a candidate label is turned into a
hypothesis and scored by an entailment model, admitting an unseen label set
without task-specific training. Yin et al. \cite{zeroshotnli} established the
formulation, and work on NLI prompt design for emotion classification
\cite{nliprompts} shows that hypothesis phrasing materially changes accuracy
across corpora, which is why we treat our template as a tunable parameter.
Generative language models offer an alternative zero-shot route
\cite{zeroshotgen}, and that is effectively what our remote tier provides. The
relevant model background is the transformer architecture \cite{attention},
BERT \cite{bert}, its distilled successors \cite{distilbert}, and the sentence
embedding models \cite{sbert, minilm} whose small size makes in-browser
inference practical.

The property we exploit is not accuracy but \emph{abstention}. An entailment
model returns a score per candidate label and cannot invent a label outside the
candidate set, unlike a generative tier, which can return a category that does
not exist. That makes a confidence gate meaningful. Cascading cheap classifiers
in front of expensive ones is a long-established efficiency pattern, from Viola
and Jones \cite{violajones} to recent work on routing queries across language
models of differing cost \cite{frugalgpt}. Our contribution is not the cascade
itself but the observation that when the cascade is deployed on a user's own
machine, the quantity it controls is not latency or cost but disclosure.

\subsection{Extracting signal from a rendered page}

Boilerplate removal, separating article text from navigation, advertising and
footers, is a mature problem; density-based approaches of the kind we implement
were characterised by Kohlsch\"utter et al. \cite{boilerplate}. Readability
scoring follows Flesch \cite{flesch}. Dominant-colour extraction from rendered
content \cite{dominantcolour} gives a cheap, language-independent signal that
survives on pages with little text. Cursor and scroll behaviour carries
information about user engagement and attention in web contexts
\cite{arapakis, cursorcikm}, which motivates our third and fourth signals; we
use them only as a bias term on an already-computed mood distribution rather
than as a primary classifier.

\subsection{Loop synthesis and audio post-processing}

Detecting a repeat point in generated audio is a structural-analysis problem.
Self-similarity over spectral features, introduced for audio segmentation by
Foote \cite{foote}, gives the candidate loop points, and beat tracking
\cite{ellis} as implemented in librosa \cite{librosa} supplies the bar grid we
snap them to. Loudness normalisation follows the integrated-loudness
formulation standardised for broadcast \cite{ebu128}, and the delivered clip is
carried in Opus \cite{opus}, whose container semantics, unlike MP3's, permit
genuinely gapless playback.

\section{Research Gaps and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:gaps}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Gaps}

Reading the two bodies of work above against each other exposes four gaps.

\textbf{G1/G5: deployment-level properties of generative systems go
unmeasured.} MusicGen, MusicLM and their successors are evaluated on audio
quality and text adherence given a prompt; how the prompt is obtained in a
running application, what it costs, and what happens during the tens of
seconds the model takes fall outside that frame, leaving little published
guidance on placing such a model behind an interactive system. The same blind
spot recurs for safety: a system that plays audio over whatever the user reads
will sometimes encounter content music is inappropriate for, and though such
safeguards are commonly claimed, we found no evaluation of one against
adversarial input, including near-miss content that must \emph{not} trigger
it.

\textbf{G2: context-aware music systems assume a library.} MusicSense
\cite{musicsense} and comparable recommenders select from a fixed catalogue,
bounding achievable specificity to whatever was licensed; the request space
needed to cover a mood, style, tempo and energy combination is rarely
quantified, and whether generation is worth its cost relative to retrieval is
asserted rather than measured.

\textbf{G3: on-device classification is treated as a latency or cost problem.}
Cascades \cite{violajones, frugalgpt} are motivated by compute and money. When
the cheap tiers run on a user's own machine and the expensive tier is a remote
API, the dominant quantity is neither: it is how much of the user's content
leaves the device. We are not aware of published work that reports an
\emph{exposure rate} as a first-class metric alongside accuracy, or exposes it
as a tunable operating curve.

\textbf{G4: generative audio models do not produce loops.} A model trained to
produce a fixed-length excerpt gives no guarantee its end joins its beginning.
Systems needing continuous ambient output must synthesise loopability after
the fact, and how much audio that costs and how much discontinuity a
crossfade actually removes is not characterised in the literature we found.

\subsection{Problem statement}

Given a rendered web page $p$, produce a continuous ambient audio stream $a(t)$
that is perceived as appropriate to $p$, subject to four constraints:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Latency.} Audio must begin within a few hundred milliseconds of a
committed decision about $p$, despite a generator with a service time of tens
to hundreds of seconds.
\item \textbf{Disclosure.} The fraction of pages whose text is transmitted off
the device must be minimised and must be measurable, and the design intends
it to be zero for pages classified as sensitive -- a target Section
\ref{sec:res-audit} tests directly and finds unmet, given a 0.655
false-negative rate on an adversarial slice.
\item \textbf{Continuity.} A clip of bounded length must repeat without an
audible seam or container gap for as long as the user remains on $p$.
\item \textbf{Restraint.} For identifiable classes of page, the correct output
is no output, and the system must be verifiable against that requirement.
\end{enumerate}

The claims we test are: (C1) the fallback-then-swap design keeps
time-to-first-audio within the latency constraint while the generator runs;
(C2) the abstaining cascade reduces exposure relative to a keyword-then-LLM
design at a measurable accuracy cost, and the thresholds expose that as a
continuous trade-off; (C3) bar-snapped loop synthesis reduces measured seam
discontinuity; (C4) generation covers a request space that retrieval of a
plausibly sized library does not; and (C5) the restraint policies fire when
they should and not otherwise. C1 and C4 are supported. C3 is qualified rather
than supported: loop synthesis measurably reduces seam discontinuity once the
loop point is chosen by the metric that matters, but the mechanism proposed
here, chroma self-similarity plus a short crossfade, does not reliably deliver
that reduction on its own (Section \ref{sec:res-loop}). C5 is answered in the
negative: the restraint mechanisms all pass their tests, but the
sensitive-content predicate they gate on misses most of an adversarial slice
(Section \ref{sec:res-audit}). C2 is answered only in part, for reasons stated
in Section \ref{sec:res-classification}.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Web2Music is a Manifest V3 Chrome extension plus one HTTP audio-generation
service. Four subsystems, referred to throughout as Features A, B, C and D,
are chained by three versioned handoffs. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the
process topology. The division of labour is dictated by browser security
boundaries as much as by design: Feature A must run in the page's content
script to reach the DOM, Feature B runs in the service worker because the
content script's origin is untrusted, and playback and ONNX inference run in an
offscreen document because Manifest V3 forbids audio in a service worker and a
page's content security policy blocks WebAssembly compilation. Only Feature D
is a network service.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.93\columnwidth]{figures/pipeline.pdf}}
\caption{Process topology and the three handoffs. Everything except Feature D
executes on the user's machine; Feature D is drawn with a dashed border to mark
the only network boundary. The dotted edge carries ONNX inference and IndexedDB
access from the content script to the offscreen document, because a page's
content security policy blocks both in the page context.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature A: page descriptor extraction}

A single function assembles a validated descriptor from six extractors. Text
extraction strips boilerplate by text-density scoring over the DOM and reads
the meta description and document language. A 384-dimension MiniLM sentence
embedding \cite{minilm} is computed in-browser through ONNX in a Web Worker
hosted by the offscreen document, with remote model loading disabled outright.
Colour extraction builds an area-weighted HSL histogram over the computed
background colours of up to 800 sampled elements, yielding dominant hues, a
scalar colour-energy term, and a representative hue, saturation and lightness
triple. A behaviour tracker exposes rolling scroll and cursor velocity in
pixels per second from throttled listeners, capped at ten and twenty events per
second respectively. Readability is a Flesch score \cite{flesch} normalised to
the unit interval. Finally a persistent IndexedDB vector store answers whether
a similar page has been seen before, so that a revisit can reuse a mood rather
than re-run the pipeline; cosine thresholds are 0.95 for a duplicate, 0.85 for
the same topic and 0.70 for a related page.

Four of these six extractors -- text, the embedding, colour and behaviour --
feed the mood and category decision and are the ``four signals'' this
paper's other claims and the signal ablation (Section
\ref{sec:res-signal-ablation}) refer to. The remaining two are not classification inputs: readability is
computed and stored in the descriptor but is not consumed by either tier and
is not ablated here; the vector store is a caching mechanism, not a signal.

Two invariants matter. Vectors from different embedding models are never
compared, because a 384-dimension MiniLM vector and a 1536-dimension
alternative describe different spaces, and comparing them returns confident
nonsense rather than an error; records are therefore namespaced by backend,
model and dimensionality. URLs are canonicalised, with tracking parameters and
fragments stripped but meaningful query strings preserved, so that one article
reachable at three URLs is one record.

Extraction is kept off the critical path: it is debounced, scheduled through
the browser's idle callback, and re-run on single-page-application navigation
and throttled DOM mutation.

\subsection{Feature B: the tiered classifier}
\label{sec:featureB}

Feature B maps the descriptor onto a thirteen-way content category and an
eleven-way mood, then onto a music profile and a text prompt. The cascade is
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/cascade.pdf}}
\caption{The three-tier classifier. Each tier carries its own acceptance
condition; a tier that cannot meet it escalates. Tier 1.5 is an entailment
model and can therefore \emph{abstain}, which is what makes its two thresholds
a continuous dial between accuracy and exposure rather than a fixed operating
point. Tier 2 is the only stage that transmits page text off the device, and it
is unreachable for pages the sensitive check has already short-circuited.}
\label{fig:cascade}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Tier 1, keyword heuristic.} A keyword map over title, description and
cleaned text produces a category. The mood distribution blends the keyword
score with an HSL colour bias, in which saturation and lightness shift valence
and arousal, and a behaviour bias, in which scroll and cursor velocity shift
arousal. If the resulting confidence reaches 0.5 the pipeline stops here, at
essentially zero cost and with nothing leaving the device. The tier is skipped
by construction for non-English pages, which is the single largest known gap in
the design.

\textbf{Tier 1.5, zero-shot NLI with abstention.} On escalation the cleaned
text is scored as an NLI premise against thirteen category hypotheses. The tier
returns a label only if the top score is at least 0.45 \emph{and} its margin
over the runner-up is at least 0.10; otherwise it abstains and escalates. Two
backends exist: a hosted entailment endpoint, and a fully local quantised ONNX
export that runs in the extension's own worker, which makes a zero-exposure
configuration possible at some accuracy cost.

\textbf{Tier 2, remote LLM.} A hosted instruction-tuned model with a structured
JSON response, an eight-second timeout, and a fall-back-to-tier-1 path on any
failure. This is the only tier that transmits page text, and the fraction of
pages reaching it is the exposure rate.

\textbf{Profile and prompt.} The committed mood is mapped to a music profile,
comprising tempo, key, energy, valence, intensity, instrument list, timbre,
reverb, ambience, style and dynamics, and thence to a generator prompt. For the
\emph{focused} mood this yields, in part: ``lo-fi study, minimal electronic,
deep focus. Instruments: piano, minimalist synth, light percussion, bass drone.
Key: D minor, 88 BPM. No vocals. Seamlessly loopable. Instrumental only.''

\textbf{The confidence window.} A newly classified mood does not take effect
immediately; it must remain stable for five seconds before a transition is
committed, so that scrolling through a link-heavy page or a brief detour does
not cause the music to lurch. As Section \ref{sec:res-latency} shows, this is
by far the largest term in the end-to-end wall clock, and it is a design
parameter rather than an implementation limitation.

\subsection{Feature D: generation, loop synthesis and caching}

Feature D is an HTTP service with a five-stage pipeline, shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:featured}: validate, prompt, generate, post-process, cache.
Validation fills every missing field with a default, so a malformed profile
degrades rather than fails.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/featured.pdf}}
\caption{Feature D. The cache is consulted before the generator, and any stage
failure diverts to a pre-generated per-mood clip rather than an error. The D4
box is the loop-synthesis stage that converts a one-shot generator output into
material that repeats; step 3 is where roughly half the generated audio is
discarded, and step 5 is where the container choice, rather than the crossfade,
is what actually makes the loop gapless.}
\label{fig:featured}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Loop synthesis.} The generator produces a one-shot clip, not a loop.
Chroma features are computed and a vectorised self-similarity curve
\cite{foote} is evaluated over candidate loop lengths, excluding anything
shorter than three seconds. The best candidate is snapped to a bar boundary
inferred from beat times \cite{ellis, librosa} under a 4/4 assumption, since
true downbeats are not available. The clip is truncated at the loop point, and
its head and tail are blended with a 250 ms equal-power crossfade (Section
\ref{sec:res-loop} justifies this width against the 50 ms default used to
produce this paper's other seam measurements) so that the loop is continuous
in energy across the seam. Loudness is normalised to
$-18$ LUFS \cite{ebu128} and the clip is exported as Ogg/Opus \cite{opus}
rather than MP3. That last choice is not aesthetic: an MP3 encoder pads the
stream, and the padding is audible as a gap on every loop iteration. The
container, not the crossfade, is what makes the loop gapless. The service also
computes a diagnostic seam discontinuity, the RMS energy and spectral-centroid
differences across the seam, both before and after the crossfade; this is the
objective measure reported in Section \ref{sec:res-loop}.

\textbf{Cache.} Requests are keyed on mood, style and key exactly, on energy,
valence and arousal to one decimal place, on a three-way tempo bucket, a
two-second duration bucket, and the export codec, so that a codec change
invalidates the cache automatically. The generation seed is deliberately
excluded: retries use fresh seeds, and including it would defeat caching
entirely. A pre-warm grid of ninety-nine mood, style and tempo-bucket
combinations is generated ahead of time.

\subsection{Feature C: extension shell and playback}

Playback lives in an offscreen document. Two alternating decks over raw audio
buffer sources, rather than a higher-level player abstraction, are required so
that loop start and end points can honour the detected loop point. They feed
four independent gain stages in series, for crossfade, user volume, ducking and
mood fade, so that un-ducking cannot erase a mood fade in progress, and then an
equaliser, reverb and analyser chain.

\textbf{Fallback-then-swap} is the mechanism that hides generation latency. On
a committed mood the client issues a request for the pre-generated fallback
clip and a generation request in parallel. The fallback clip, correct in mood
and generic in content, begins playing immediately; when generation completes,
the generated clip is crossfaded in on the second deck. One abort controller
per request means a new mood cancels a stale generation rather than letting it
arrive late and overwrite a now-incorrect track.

\textbf{Restraint.} Three behaviours are deliberate refusals to produce output.
Pages classified as sensitive, covering crisis, grief, self-harm and violence,
produce silence, and their text is short-circuited before either LLM tier. If
the active tab is itself audible the extension mutes, and if a background tab
is audible it ducks, so that it never competes with media the user chose.
Payment, banking and checkout pages, and all browser-internal URLs, are
bypassed entirely.

\textbf{Telemetry} is a local-only ring buffer. Every URL is hashed with
SHA-256 before storage and nothing leaves the machine unless the user
explicitly exports it. This bound is thinner than it sounds: URLs are
public, so a bare SHA-256 hash is dictionary-recoverable against a modest
precomputed table of common ones -- the guarantee is that plaintext never
leaves the device, not that a stored hash cannot be reversed to the URL it
came from. An HMAC keyed with a per-install local secret, rather than a
bare hash, would close this gap without changing what leaves the device;
it has not been implemented here.

\subsection{Evaluation design and provenance}
\label{sec:res-provenance}

The evaluation is organised as five studies. S1 is a systems benchmark covering
extraction cost on real sites, generation latency by request class and server
stage, and cost accounting in tokens, bytes and currency. S2 is a
classification accuracy and tier ablation over seven configurations run offline
against a frozen corpus. S3 covers audio and loop quality, objectively over
every clip on disk and subjectively through a forced-choice listening test. S4
is a controlled within-subjects user study with four conditions: silence, a
fixed playlist, a shuffled condition in which the real generator is driven by a
mood drawn from a \emph{different} page, and the adaptive system. S5 is an
in-the-wild deployment. The shuffled condition is what would make the central
perceptual claim falsifiable, since it holds generator, loop processing,
crossfades and latency profile constant and varies only whether the music
matches the page.

S1, the objective half of S3, the retrieval baseline and the restraint audit
have been executed and are reported here. S4 and S5 require institutional
ethics approval that has not been obtained, and no human-subjects data has been
collected. S2's tier-ablation table now rests on 254 curation-time-labelled
pages rather than 18, but that label is still a single source rather than the
three-annotator, agreement-checked one this paper otherwise requires, so what
we report for it remains explicitly preliminary. The corpus itself has since
been annotated by three independent annotators, and the resulting
inter-annotator agreement is reported below.

Every metric the evaluation may print is declared in a machine-readable
registry with its definition, units, producing script and output field. Each
figure and table generator declares the metric identifiers it consumes, and the
build driver rejects any generator naming an undeclared metric and reports
generators whose input data is absent as blocked, rather than allowing them to
produce an empty or imputed plot. At the time of writing the driver builds
seven of thirteen declared artefacts and blocks six. This apparatus is worth
describing because it is the mechanism that surfaced two of the four negative
results below: a generator refused to print a number its input did not support.

\textbf{Corpus.} The classification corpus is stored as extracted page
descriptors rather than URLs, because live pages change underneath a benchmark
and destroy replication. A capture of 260 pages exists, of which 255 were
captured, three were correctly bypassed and two failed navigation, across seven
deliberately adversarial slices: 182 general, 15 vocabulary-mismatch pages
whose words do not match their category, 15 non-English, 15 mixed-genre, 12
sensitive, 15 sensitive near-miss pages that must \emph{not} trigger silence,
and 6 bypass. No bypass violations and no duplicate content hashes were
recorded. Coverage across the thirteen content categories is uneven by
construction rather than a flat approximately 20 pages each: counts range from
14 (Mythological) to 35 (Health), since curated availability differs by
category and the original 600-page target (approximately 45 per category) was
not reached. The corpus has since been annotated by three independent
annotators; the resulting agreement is reported below.

\textbf{Hardware.} All timings are from a single CPU laptop running Windows 11
with the generation service in a local container alongside a PostgreSQL cache.
No GPU configuration was measured.

\textbf{Availability.} The system's source code, the extraction and
generation pipelines, the analysis scripts behind every table and figure
in this paper, and the corpus of extracted page descriptors are available
at \url{https://github.com/AyushK0808/web2music}.

\subsection{Annotation agreement and human ceiling}
\label{sec:res-agreement}

Three independent annotators labelled all 255 captured pages for content
category (13-way), mood (11-way, framed as a preference rather than a fact),
and sensitivity (binary), without conferring, using a purpose-built annotation
tool and analysed with a bootstrap Krippendorff's-alpha procedure (nominal
$\alpha$, 95\% CI over 2000 resamples of units, and leave-one-annotator-out
human ceiling). Table \ref{tab:agreement} reports the result for each label
set.

\label{tab:agreement}
\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

Category  & 0.635 & [0.578, 0.689] & 0.621 & below 0.667 \\

All three label sets fall below the 0.667 minimum the plan sets for drawing
conclusions, and none reach the 0.80 threshold for good agreement. Per that
same policy, we report this as a finding rather than treat it as a defect to
fix by re-annotating, and the mood result is the more important of the two to
read this way. At $\alpha=0.195$, three annotators working independently from
the same instructions are close to chance-level agreement on which of eleven
moods a page calls for. The system's primary output is an eleven-way mood.
That three humans cannot agree on it is not a footnote to the mood
classifier's accuracy; it is evidence about the task itself, and it says the
task, as specified, may not have a single defensible answer per page. Category
agreement (0.635) is closer to usable but still short of it.

Both ceilings in Table \ref{tab:agreement} sit below their own label's
$\alpha$ -- 0.621 against 0.635 for category, 0.174 against 0.195 for
mood -- which looks like an error but follows from what the two statistics
actually measure. The ceiling is a strict, conservative hit rate: does the
held-out annotator's label exactly match the majority of the other two,
with any tie between those two others counted as a miss rather than a
partial credit, by deliberate design so the ceiling is never inflated. With
only three annotators, ``majority of the other two'' is frequently a tie,
and every tie is a guaranteed miss regardless of how close the three labels
actually were. $\alpha$, by contrast, is a chance-corrected coefficient
averaged continuously across all pairwise comparisons, with no majority
requirement and no all-or-nothing penalty for a near-miss. A ceiling built
from strict, tie-punished binary hits has no guaranteed ordering against a
smoother, chance-corrected coefficient, and sensitivity's ceiling sitting
\emph{above} its own $\alpha$ later in this table is the same asymmetry
running the other way.

To ask whether that disagreement is a fine-grained labelling problem rather
than a disagreement about the underlying affect, we collapsed the eleven mood
labels onto Russell's valence-arousal circumplex two different ways and
recomputed $\alpha$ on the same three annotators' raw labels under each. We
name the four quadrants by axis sign throughout -- positive-valence/
high-arousal, negative-valence/high-arousal, negative-valence/low-arousal,
positive-valence/low-arousal -- rather than by an exemplar mood word, because
an exemplar can end up outside its own quadrant and read as an error rather
than as the finding it is.

The first mapping uses this system's own per-mood valence hint and BPM-range
midpoint, the same encoding used elsewhere in the pipeline, with a median
split. It gives $\alpha=0.121$, 95\% CI [0.057, 0.186] -- lower than the
eleven-way figure, not higher -- under heavily skewed membership: the
positive-valence/high-arousal quadrant alone accounts for 466 of 697
annotator judgements (66.9 percent).

Because that mapping tests this system's own taxonomy's geometry rather than
Russell's, we repeated the collapse using the NRC Valence-Arousal-Dominance
lexicon's published word-level norms~\cite{nrcvad}, splitting each axis at
the lexicon's own 0--1 scale midpoint, 0.5, not a median fit to these eleven
words specifically. (One mood word, ``focused,'' has no direct entry; we
substitute its lemma, ``focus.'') Under these norms, no mood word falls in
the negative-valence/high-arousal quadrant at all, including the word
``tense'' itself, which the norms rate as low-arousal -- a coverage gap in an
eleven-word taxonomy built without reference to a circumplex, not an
artefact of the mapping. Membership across the three occupied quadrants is
289, 222 and 186 of 697 judgements: less skewed than the first mapping, but
the largest quadrant still holds 41.5 percent. This mapping gives
$\alpha=0.300$, 95\% CI [0.231, 0.365].

As a sanity check on the recomputation itself, we re-ran the identical
procedure on the original eleven-way labels, with no quadrant collapse. It
returns exactly 0.195, matching the raw figure reported above. This confirms
the collapse step, not a bug in the recomputation, is what moves the number
under either mapping.

The two mappings disagree about which way coarsening moves agreement: the
system's own geometry makes it worse, published norms make it nearly double.
Both start from the same three annotators' raw labels; only the placement of
eleven words on two axes differs. That the answer flips with the mapping is
itself informative, not a nuisance to resolve before reporting a number -- it
means quadrant-level agreement for this taxonomy is sensitive to exactly
where the words sit, a second and independent argument that the affective
target is underdetermined, on top of whichever $\alpha$ is closer to correct.
A boundary check on the NRC-VAD placements sharpens this: five of the eleven
words -- nostalgic, curious, tense, uplifting and neutral -- sit within 0.1
of a 0.5 split on at least one axis, so nearly half the taxonomy's quadrant
memberships are close calls rather than clear regions, and a different norm
source could plausibly move several of them to a different quadrant.

Read together, the more defensible conclusion has three parts. The
eleven-word taxonomy is over-specified relative to what annotators agree on
at the fine grain. It is also missing coverage of at least one region
Russell's circumplex predicts, a gap the NRC mapping surfaces and the
system's own mapping does not. And the coarse-grained agreement figure itself
depends on which valence-arousal source supplies the collapse, so neither
0.121 nor 0.300 should be read as \emph{the} answer to how well quadrant-level
affect is agreed upon -- only as two bounds on a target that moves with the
measurement. Mood should continue to be reported as a stylistic preference
the system offers, not a label with a defensible ground truth at any
resolution tested here.

Sensitivity is the most consequential label for the system's abstention
policy, and its two statistics diverge: $\alpha=0.419$ is below threshold,
while the leave-one-out human ceiling is the highest of the three (0.775).
This is consistent with the label's skew — 219 of 250 resolved units (87.6\%)
are majority-labelled not-sensitive — since chance-corrected agreement
statistics are depressed by a lopsided marginal distribution even when raw
annotator agreement is substantial, precisely because chance agreement is
already high under that skew. The two numbers should be read together rather
than either alone: annotators mostly agree on individual pages, but that
agreement is not distinguishable enough from chance under $\alpha$'s
definition to clear the plan's own bar. Any classifier-accuracy claim drawn
from this corpus, including the tier-ablation rebuild in Section
\ref{sec:res-classification}, must be interpreted against these ceilings
rather than against a naively perfect one.

\subsection{Which signals earn their cost}
\label{sec:res-signal-ablation}

Contribution 1 claims four signals: text, colour, behaviour and an embedding.
We ran a per-signal offline ablation against the full 255-page corpus,
removing colour, behaviour and the embedding in turn and comparing the
resulting category and mood against the unablated descriptor.

Category never changes. Across the full descriptor and each of the three
removal conditions, the predicted category is identical on every page.
Category is decided by text alone under the offline heuristic tiers,
regardless of what else is present in the descriptor.

Mood is more textured, but the same pattern holds for two of the three
non-text signals. Removing the behaviour profile or the embedding changes
mood on zero pages. Removing colour changes mood on 7.5
percent of pages, and stripping down to text alone changes it on 10.2
percent, so colour accounts for most of what little the non-text signals
contribute, and behaviour and the embedding currently contribute nothing
measurable to either output.

Read plainly: the four-signal descriptor is, under this ablation, a text
descriptor with three additional inputs, of which one, colour, moves the
output measurably, and only for mood. This was run offline, where every page
is decided by a keyword default or a fixed rule rather than by either model
tier, so it bounds what colour can be contributing rather than measuring its
contribution under the shipped cascade; a live-tier repeat of this ablation
has not been done and remains future work (\S\ref{sec:future}).

dominates every cache-hit response and is the largest addressable inefficiency

The real-time factor is not flat. It holds at 1.23$\times$ across 5 and 10 s,
then climbs to 1.75$\times$ by 28 s -- a 42 percent increase in cost per
second of delivered audio between the shortest and longest requests in the
sweep. A linear fit against requested duration is a reasonable first pass
($r=0.987$), but its residuals are systematically signed (negative in the
middle of the range, positive at both ends), which is the signature of the
wrong functional form rather than noise; a power-law fit does better
(generation time $\propto \text{duration}^{1.185}$, against the $\propto
\text{duration}^{1.0}$ that the $\sim$50-tokens/sec budget in
\S\ref{sec:res-loop} assumes). MusicGen's autoregressive decoding growing
mildly superlinearly with sequence length -- consistent with attention cost
over a growing key-value cache -- is the natural explanation, though this
sweep measures the effect, not its cause. The practical consequence is that
the token-budget assumption used elsewhere in this paper to convert requested
duration into expected generation cost is a mild underestimate at the long
end and should be read as a floor, not a point estimate. Fig.~\ref{fig:f2}
plots the CPU-laptop run and should not be redrawn from Table
\ref{tab:duration-scaling}, which is a different machine; what it needs
instead is a same-hardware repeat of its own duration sweep at $n\geq3$ per
cell, since its two open-marker cells and the shape claim built on them are
still exactly as thin as the qualification above this paragraph describes.

generations, stands unchanged for every CPU-laptop number in this paper --
the 139 s headline and Tables \ref{tab:d-latency}--\ref{tab:d-stages} chief
among them -- and this sweep does not touch it. What it does establish, on
its own hardware, is that the real-time factor is not constant, which is a
qualitative claim about MusicGen's decoding behaviour that a same-hardware
CPU sweep would be expected to reproduce, though that reproduction has not
been run. That same-hardware repeat remains outstanding on the original
Windows 11 CPU laptop (\S\ref{sec:res-latency}); a CPU-only run on the
Apple Silicon machine used elsewhere in this section would not substitute
for it, being architecturally distinct from the original hardware.

\textbf{Discussion.} The interesting engineering result is not that generation
is slow, which is well known, but that a two-stage fallback-then-swap makes a
139 s generator feel immediate, at the cost of the first seconds of every mood
being generic rather than specific. That cost is nearly invisible, because a
listener who has not heard the specific clip cannot miss it. The corollary is
that the dominant term in the end-to-end wall clock is not the model at all: it
is the five-second confidence window, which we chose, and which exists to stop
the music lurching on a brief detour. Reporting a single time-to-music figure
for this system would therefore be actively misleading, attributing a design
decision about stability to an implementation limitation about speed. We report
the two separately and suggest that any always-on adaptive-media system do the
same.

fixture is built so tier-1's colour and keyword heuristics match nothing,
forcing more extraction cycles before a mood commits (max 5 vs.\ a median of
3). Ambiguous pages cost real, measurable browser latency, additional to the
escalation cost already quantified in Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}.

Two limitations bound this measurement. Feature D and the classify-proxy were
both stubbed, so the 75.7 percent real-corpus escalation rate from
Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} is not reflected here, and the true network cost
of escalation on a real page remains unmeasured end to end. The harness also
reuses one persistent browser context across every site and repeat, so a
generation response arriving after its site's wait deadline could in
principle be attributed to a later site's telemetry window; all 54 Feature D
round-trip times cluster tightly around the configured 139.9 s delay with no
anomalously short values, which argues against this happening, though the
harness does not positively rule it out.
\subsection{Cost and cache behaviour}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Cost accounting was recomputed over the full S2 corpus (255 of 260 pages; two
failed captures and three bypassed internal pages have no extracted content
and are excluded rather than imputed), superseding the earlier 18-page
smoke-set figures. Unit costs are properties of the models and their
pricing, not the corpus, so per-page figures scale with escalation rate --
and the real corpus escalates far more than the smoke set implied: 75.7
percent of pages reach the keyword tier's ceiling, against 22.2 percent on
the 18-page subset Table \ref{tab:tiers} still reports pending the 600-page
annotation (D-01/D-02). At the observed rate the cascade sends a mean of
241.5 LLM tokens per page (319.1 on escalating pages), performs 9.84
zero-shot forward passes per page on average, and transmits 1642 bytes per
page (979 LLM, 663 zero-shot proxy) -- 0.0132 USD per thousand pages at the
tier-2 provider's published price (re-verified 2026-08-17), rising to 0.0174
USD if every page escalated.

That figure changes what the cascade contribution claims: even the
pessimistic number is negligible, so nobody needs this cascade to save money.
It is worth building because it controls how much of the user's browsing
leaves their machine, a quantity with no price at which it becomes acceptable
to stop measuring -- the substance of gap G3, that the same mechanism the
efficiency literature motivates by cost is, on a user's own device, a
disclosure control.

Audio cost is dominated by cache behaviour. Over a simulated 200-page browsing
session against the ninety-nine-cell pre-warm grid, the expected cache hit rate
is 57 percent under a uniform mood distribution, 65 percent under mild skew and
83 percent under heavy skew. Which regime real browsing occupies is what the
in-the-wild study would measure, and browsing is almost certainly not uniform.

backend, not the local ONNX export, so it is itself an off-device call: every

backend, the abstaining cascade does not reduce how many pages leave the
device at all; it only decides which hosted provider a given page's text goes
to, and for roughly half of the escalating pages, whether it goes to
\emph{both}.

The A7 sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:f5}, twelve minScore $\times$ minMargin cells)
confirms this is not particular to the shipped thresholds. Macro-F1 ranges

Groq and the zero-shot proxy and moves the smaller, LLM-specific exposure
number substantially; it does not move the number that answers ``does this
page's text leave the machine at all.'' That narrower number is still useful:
Section \ref{sec:res-cost}'s cost model and the LLM-specific risk of a
free-text prompt reaching a generative remote model both scale with it, not
with proxy usage. But it is not the same claim as exposure reduction in
general, and reporting only the LLM-only number, as an earlier draft of this
section did, would have overstated what the shipped cascade achieves with
this backend.

Claim C2 is therefore answered in part, and less favourably than the
escalation counts alone suggest. The cascade reduces LLM-specific exposure and
cost substantially and the threshold pair genuinely dials that number
continuously; it does not, with the hosted zero-shot backend measured here,
reduce total disclosure below the keyword-to-LLM baseline. The one
configuration that would change that -- A6/A6b, the local quantised ONNX
zero-shot backend, which the 18-page preliminary run suggested could match
A4's accuracy at half its exposure -- has not yet been run against the full
260-page corpus; the zero-exposure operating point this section's future
scope depends on remains unverified at scale.

\textbf{Discussion.} The generalisable pattern is a free heuristic, a local
model permitted to abstain, and a remote model as a last resort, with the
abstention gates as the dial. Turning them up sends more pages to the remote
model and buys accuracy; turning them down keeps text on the device and costs
accuracy -- \emph{provided} the model being turned down is actually local.
With a hosted middle tier, the same knob mostly redistributes disclosure
rather than reducing it, which is a materially different claim to make to a
user. Because the middle tier is an entailment model rather than a generative
one, it also cannot hallucinate a category outside its candidate set, which is
a robustness property and not merely a cost property. A generative middle tier
would not have it.

\textbf{Ground-truth sensitivity.} Table \ref{tab:tiers} scores against
curation-time, single-source category labels for all 254 pages. Three
independent annotators have since labelled this same corpus; their
majority vote reaches only $\alpha=0.635$ for category -- itself short of the
0.667 threshold this paper treats as usable -- which raises the question of
how much the accuracy figures above depend on whose labelling is scored
against, not only on the classifier. Re-scoring every configuration, including
A5 and the twelve-cell A7 sweep, against the 208 pages with a resolved
three-annotator majority category (47 of the 255 captured pages have no such
majority and are excluded rather than guessed at) gives keyword-only
.197/.297 accuracy/macro-F1, materially unchanged; LLM-only .764/.731, up 7.9
points from .685/.678; keyword-to-LLM .760/.705, up 9.1 points from
.669/.677; and the full cascade (A5) .683/.683, up only 6.1 points from
.622/.656. The correction moves every LLM-involving tier in the same
direction, which argues for curation-time labelling noise over one tier being
specifically miscalibrated. It does not move them by the same amount, though:
A5 gains distinctly less than A3 or A4 do, and that differential, not the
noise itself, is what widens the accuracy gap this section reports rather
than narrowing it. Under the corrected label the full cascade trails the
keyword-to-LLM design by 7.7 points of accuracy and 2.2 of macro-F1, wider
than Table \ref{tab:tiers}'s 4.7-point figure precisely because A4 gained
more from the correction than A5 did. A paired bootstrap over the 208
per-page correctness vectors (10{,}000 resamples) puts that 7.7-point gap at
a 95\% interval of [2.9, 12.5] points. McNemar's test on the same pairing --
23 pages A4 gets right that A5 misses, against 7 where the reverse holds --
gives $\chi^2(1)=7.50$, $p<0.01$. The gap is real and asymmetric in the
predicted direction, but the interval is wide enough that a different
208-page draw could move the point estimate meaningfully; it is reported here
as an estimate with uncertainty attached, not a settled number. The re-scored
A7 sweep confirms the disclosure finding above is not an artefact of the
single-source label: macro-F1 ranges from .650 to .695 across the twelve
cells, LLM-only exposure ranges from .115 to .572, and total off-device rate
holds at .76 throughout, reproducing the pattern in Fig.~\ref{fig:f5} exactly
under the stronger label. Table \ref{tab:tiers} underestimates accuracy at
every LLM-involving tier, understates the cascade's specific accuracy cost,
and gets the disclosure story right regardless.

support it.

\textbf{The crossfade helps less than expected, and not always.} The median
paired improvement is 0.93 dB, and the crossfade \emph{worsens} the measured
seam on 41.1 percent of clips. Spectral-centroid discontinuity rises at the
median, from 441 to 557 Hz, while falling at the tail, from 2017 to 1322 Hz:
the crossfade reliably clips the worst seams but perturbs already-good ones. An
earlier run over a subset of 116 clips reported a median improvement of 3.82
dB; the larger sample does not support that figure, and we report the larger
one. A 50 ms window is short relative to the musical events at a bar boundary,
and we test both remaining hypotheses directly: a longer crossfade window, and
selecting the loop point to minimise measured seam rather than to maximise
chroma self-similarity.

Both help, and the second helps far more than the first. Table
\ref{tab:crossfade-sweep} sweeps the crossfade width from 50 to 500 ms at the
production loop point: the median seam energy discontinuity falls
monotonically, 3.46 to 2.90 dB, a 16 percent reduction, and spectral-centroid
discontinuity falls faster still through 250 ms before ticking back up at
500 ms, consistent with a wider window eventually re-admitting a second
musical event into the blend. Widening the window is a real but modest lever.
Replacing the selection rule is not: searching the same similarity curve's
local peaks and the bar-snapped candidates for the point that minimises
\emph{measured} post-crossfade seam, rather than trusting chroma similarity as
a proxy for it, moves the loop point on 9 of 10 clips tested and cuts the
median energy discontinuity from 3.46 to 0.33 dB, an order of magnitude. Chroma
self-similarity and audible seam smoothness are evidently not the same
quantity: two windows can match well on pitch-class content while still
landing on different phases of the beat or differing in transient content, and
the crossfade only smooths what the selection rule hands it. This result is
secondary evidence, n=10 usable of 11 real fallback clips re-cut by this
script's own call into the production detector, not a re-run over the 141-clip
population behind Table \ref{tab:seam}, because the pipeline does not retain
the pre-loop-cut audio those clips were cut from; the direction and size of
the effect are large enough, though, that we would be surprised if it did not
replicate at scale.

\textbf{Half the requested audio is discarded.} The delivered clip retains a
median of 50.3 percent of its requested duration, because the loop point is
chosen for musical self-similarity and everything after it is thrown away.
Requesting 28 s reliably yields about 14 s. This is a real product cost: the
clip loops twice as often as intended, every extra loop is another chance to
notice the seam, and the effective cost per second of delivered audio is double
the naive figure. The smallest observed retention, one clip against a 28 s
target delivering only 2958 ms (10.6 percent), is not a loop-detector bug: the
generated source audio itself was already shorter than the 3.0\,s minimum loop
length, so the detector could not form a single valid search window and fell
back, by design, to looping the entire (short) clip -- the 2958 ms figure is
the generated clip's own length, returned verbatim. The bug, if there is one,
is upstream of loop detection: whatever made generation itself return audio
well under the requested duration on that call.

cache prewarming was still rebuilding in the background for the early portion
of this run, competing with the sweep's own requests for the single generation
worker, and the 5 s and 10 s cells' wall-clock times here are visibly inflated
by that contention. A separate, uncontended sweep against an already-warm

comparable to this paper's CPU-laptop latency figures elsewhere
(\S\ref{sec:res-latency}, Table \ref{tab:duration-scaling}), supplies the

with requested duration (real-time factor 1.23$\times$ at 5--10 s,
1.75$\times$ at 28 s), so shorter requests are cheaper per second of audio,
not just in absolute terms. Combined with this sweep's finding that shorter
requests are not reliably \emph{worse} on retention or seam quality -- the
15 s cell has both the lowest retention and the lowest loop-cost in Table
\ref{tab:clip-length} -- there is no quality argument for the 28 s default
over something shorter, and a mild
latency argument against it.

Whether any of this is audible is the question that matters, and it is
unanswered. A forced-choice listening harness with twenty-two seeded stimulus
pairs and a scoring script has been built and validated end to end, but only
against simulated listeners, which carries no information about human
perception. No claim about seam audibility is made here.

\textbf{Discussion.} Making a one-shot generator loop requires three things.
Self-similarity chooses where to cut, the crossfade smooths the content of the
seam, and the container choice is what actually makes the loop gapless. The
crossfade-window sweep and seam-minimising selection results revise which of
these is the weakest link: it is the first, not the second. Widening the
crossfade window buys a modest, monotonic improvement, but re-choosing the cut
point by the metric that is actually meant to be small, rather than by a proxy
for it, buys an order of magnitude more. A 50 ms crossfade was never going to
compensate for a loop point chosen on the wrong criterion. The first step is
also expensive in the sense the design did not anticipate: it discards half
the requested audio to buy self-similarity that, on this evidence, does not
reliably deliver the seam quality it is a proxy for. This is the concrete
content of gap G4: the post-hoc loopability route works, but at a 50 percent
yield, and the discontinuity reduction it delivers depends more on how the cut
point is chosen than on how the seam is subsequently smoothed. A generator
that could be conditioned to produce loop-closed material directly would
dominate all of it.

The mechanisms pass; the predicate does not. Against a 47-page adversarial

modes, the sensitive-content detector records a false-negative rate of 0.655
and a false-positive rate of 0.167. The failures are systematic rather than
noise. Seven of the nineteen false negatives are vocabulary outside the term
list entirely: pages on addiction, miscarriage, terminal illness, overdose,
workplace harassment, homelessness and post-incarceration reentry are not
detected at all, because the detector is a term list and those terms are not
on it. Four are euphemism, pages that describe self-harm, suicidal ideation or
domestic violence without ever naming the thing directly. Four are non-English,
missed entirely by the same language gate that skips tier 1. The remaining four
are a failure mode the smaller seed slice could not surface at all: genuinely
sensitive pages that mention exactly one term from the ambiguous list, one
short of the two-distinct-term threshold the detector requires before it will
act on ambiguous vocabulary alone. The false positives
did not grow with the slice: the same three near-misses the original 20-page
seed found, a page on grief-counselling degree programmes, a psychology
textbook, and an engineering article using the word ``trauma'', are still the
only three, and six additional near-miss pages added specifically to stress
this side of the boundary produced no new ones.

This answers C5 in the negative, and it is the finding with the clearest
consequence for anyone deploying such a system. A keyword list is not a
sensitive-content detector. A system that promises silence on crisis content
and delivers it 34.5 percent of the time has not implemented the policy it
claims. The architecture is sound, in that the short-circuit is in the right
place and provably fires, but the predicate feeding it is inadequate, and the
remedy is a classifier rather than more keywords. The entailment tier already
loaded for category classification can score a sensitivity hypothesis at near
zero marginal cost.

More generally, this is the substance of gap G1/G5. For an always-on generative
system, the quality of the abstention policy is as much part of the design as
the quality of the generation, and unlike aesthetic quality it is directly
measurable. Measuring it is what produced the most uncomfortable result in this
paper: every mechanism passes its test, while the predicate feeding the most
important mechanism fails on nearly two-thirds of an adversarial slice. That gap
between ``the architecture is correct'' and ``the policy is delivered'' is
invisible to any evaluation that tests only the mechanism.

generation. This supports C4. The bucket granularity is stated deliberately:
coarser buckets would flatter the generation argument, so we chose defensible
ones and publish them alongside the number.

\subsection{Prompt engineering ablation}
\label{sec:res-promptablation}

Feature B constructs a long, structured prompt for the generator (Section
\ref{sec:featureB}); Feature D falls back to a shorter template of its own
when no such prompt is supplied. Whether the engineered prompt earns its
extra complexity over that fallback, or over no engineering at all, has not
previously been tested. We compare three conditions holding mood, duration
and generation seed policy fixed: \textbf{P1}, a bare mood word with no
engineering; \textbf{P2}, Feature D's own fallback template
(\texttt{d2\_prompt.build\_prompt}); and \textbf{P3}, Feature B's engineered
production prompt. Each condition was run for five representative moods
(calm, focused, tense, energetic, sad) at three repeats each, for 45
generations with no cache hits, since every request carries a unique nonce.
All 45 completed with no fallbacks and no request errors.

\textbf{The engineered prompt does not win.} P3, the longer production
prompt Feature B actually sends in the deployed system, has the worst
median seam discontinuity of the three conditions, and this is not an
artefact of one outlier: its best individual result (1.98 dB) is still
worse than the best result either P1 or P2 achieved. On duration retention
the picture is noisier -- all three conditions range from roughly 0.2 to
0.9 across individual clips, and the median difference is small against
that spread. This runs against the implicit assumption behind building an
engineered template in the first place, though at $n=15$ per condition the
seam result is the more defensible of the two and the duration-retention
gap should be read cautiously.

\textbf{What this does not show.} As with the loop-quality results in
Section \ref{sec:res-loop}, an objective measure is not a perceptual one.
Prompt specificity plausibly affects genre adherence, musical coherence, or
perceived quality in ways seam discontinuity and duration retention cannot
capture, and no listening test accompanies this ablation. Whether P3 is
worth keeping in production, on grounds this ablation cannot see, is not
answered here.

\textbf{A hardware caveat.} This ablation ran on a Tesla T4 GPU rather than
the single CPU laptop used for every other latency and generation figure in
this paper (Section \ref{sec:res-provenance}, ``Hardware''). Median
generation time here was approximately 18 s per clip across all three
conditions, against a median of 139 s on CPU in Table \ref{tab:d-latency}.
That gap is expected and is not itself a finding: it should not be read as
a GPU-configuration result in the sense discussed in Section
\ref{sec:res-latency}, since sample size, moods and harness differ between
the two runs. A controlled CPU-vs-GPU comparison holding those fixed remains
future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We presented Web2Music, a browser extension that conditions generative ambient
music on the page being read, and reported a systems evaluation of it: a
four-signal page descriptor costing 86 ms at the median across 105 real
sites, excluding the embedding stage which was stubbed for this measurement,
of which an ablation shows only one non-text signal, colour, moves either
output measurably; a three-tier abstaining classifier whose thresholds turn
LLM-specific exposure into a continuous dial and which classifies a thousand
pages for about 1.3 cents, though with the hosted zero-shot backend measured
here that dial redistributes disclosure between providers rather than
reducing how much text leaves the device in total; a fallback-then-swap
playback design that hides a 139 s generator behind a 7 ms placeholder; and
loop synthesis that makes a one-shot generator's output repeat without a
container gap.

Three results run against the design's own assumptions and are reported as
such. The equal-power crossfade removes a median of only 0.93 dB of seam
discontinuity and worsens the measured seam on 41 percent of clips. Half the
generated audio is discarded to find a loop point, so a 28 s request yields
about 14 s of delivered material. And the sensitive-content detector, whose
refusal mechanism is architecturally correct and fully covered by passing
tests, misses 65.5 percent of an adversarial crisis slice because its
predicate is a keyword list rather than a classifier.

A fourth result is not about this system specifically. Three independent
annotators agree on which of eleven moods a page calls for at
$\alpha=0.195$, close to chance, and collapsing that label onto Russell's
valence-arousal quadrants using published word norms raises agreement to only
$0.300$, still short of a usable threshold (\S\ref{sec:res-agreement}).
Coarsening helps but does not rescue the task, and the taxonomy is also
missing coverage of at least one region a circumplex model predicts. Any
system that conditions media on inferred page or context mood inherits this:
the affective target is underdetermined by three independent human readings
at both the resolution such a system is likely to report and the coarser one
Russell's model offers.

The claim that page-conditioned music is perceived as fitting the page better
than page-agnostic music remains untested. The study that would test it,
including the shuffled condition that makes it falsifiable, is designed and
instrumented but has not been run.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{No human-subjects data.} The central perceptual claim is untested.
Everything established here is about the system, not about its effect on
people. The shuffled condition that would isolate conditioning from generation
has never been run with a participant, and the forced-choice loop test has been
validated only against simulated listeners.

\textbf{Construct validity of page mood.} What mood a page ``should'' have is a
preference, not a fact. We frame the annotation task as which of the eleven
moods an annotator would least object to hearing while reading the page, and we
hold that any reported accuracy must be printed alongside the inter-annotator
ceiling. Section \ref{sec:res-agreement} now supplies one for each label --
0.621 for category and 0.775 for sensitivity -- and the classification
results in Section \ref{sec:res-classification} should be read against them.
Mood has no comparably useful ceiling: at 0.174 it sits below the raw
eleven-way agreement figure itself, which is a separate argument against
treating mood accuracy as a claim this paper makes at all.

\textbf{Preliminary classification results.} Table \ref{tab:tiers} now rests
on 254 scored pages rather than 18, and the threshold sweep in
Fig.~\ref{fig:f5} covers a full twelve-cell grid rather than none. The
three-annotator, agreement-checked label \S\ref{sec:limitations} otherwise
requires now exists (\S\ref{sec:res-agreement}), and every configuration in
Table \ref{tab:tiers}, including A5 and the twelve-cell A7 sweep, has been
re-scored against it: LLM-involving accuracy rises, but not uniformly --
6.1 points for the full cascade (A5), 7.9 for LLM-only (A3), and 9.1 for
keyword-to-LLM (A4), over the single-source figures the table reports. A5
gains distinctly less than the other two, and that differential is what
widens, rather than narrows, the cascade's accuracy cost relative to A4: the
corrected gap is an estimated 7.7 points, with a 95\% paired-bootstrap
interval of [2.9, 12.5] (\S\ref{sec:res-classification}), not the table's flat
4.7. Table \ref{tab:tiers} itself is therefore a conservative floor on
accuracy rather than a precise estimate, and the 7.7-point figure should be
read as an estimate with a wide interval attached, not a settled number,
though the disclosure story it tells is unchanged under the stronger label.
Separately,
A6/A6b (the local zero-shot backend, the one configuration that could reduce
total disclosure rather than redistribute it) has not been run at this scale,
which remains the more consequential open gap.

\textbf{English-only.} Tier 1 is skipped by construction on non-English pages,
and the sensitive detector missed the one non-English sensitive page in the
audit. Both are consequences of the same language gate. The pipeline nominally
handles other languages by escalating; whether it handles them well is
unmeasured.

\textbf{Single hardware configuration.} Every latency figure comes from one CPU
laptop. A GPU configuration, on which the fallback-then-swap design plausibly
stops being necessary, and a cache-cold comparison, are both unmeasured.

\textbf{Small samples inside the systems results.} Generation percentiles rest
on three requests. This is stated at the point of use rather than aggregated
away. The restraint audit
now rests on forty-seven pages rather than the original twenty, but that is
still small next to the 141-clip and 105-website samples elsewhere in the
paper, and the false-negative categories with the fewest examples (four pages
each for non-English and for the single-ambiguous-term case) are the ones
whose rates should be trusted least. The concurrency figure measures the
request and cache path rather than generator batching, because every request
in that sweep was a cache hit.

\textbf{Cache state confounds latency and audio together.} A cache hit changes
both the response time and the returned audio, since an identical clip recurs.
Any user study must record cache state per session and should probably force
fresh generation, accepting the latency cost.

\textbf{Ethics and measurement boundaries.} No human-subjects data was
collected; the planned studies require institutional approval that has not been
obtained. Three design decisions are ethically load-bearing: page text is
transmitted off-device only on escalation and never for pages marked sensitive;
telemetry is local-only, URL-hashed at write time and exported only on an
explicit user action; and crisis content produces silence rather than music. We
regard the 65.5 percent false-negative rate on that last policy, measured now
on a slice more than double the size that first surfaced it, as a defect to be
fixed before any deployment to participants, not a limitation to be noted in
passing. Separately, uninstall rate is not measurable in this build, because
the browser deletes extension-local storage on uninstall and the only surviving
hook would require a network call that the local-only guarantee forbids;
voluntary toggle-off is a proxy and not a substitute.

\section{Future Scope}
\label{sec:future}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Complete the perceptual evaluation.} The immediate priority is
institutional ethics approval, followed by the four-condition within-subjects
study and the in-the-wild deployment. The analysis plan is pre-specified:
ordinal outcomes through cumulative-link mixed models with random intercepts
for participant and page, comprehension treated as an equivalence claim rather
than a null result, and multiple-comparison control within a declared
confirmatory family. Sample size is set at 56 to survive attrition at a
small-to-medium effect size.

\textbf{Run the local zero-shot backend at scale.} A6/A6b
(\S\ref{sec:res-classification}) is the one configuration that could reduce
total disclosure rather than redistribute it between hosted providers, and it
has still only been measured on the 18-page preliminary subset. It matters
more than that subset size suggests: the real 255-page corpus escalates at
75.7 percent
(\S\ref{sec:res-cost}), not the 22.2 percent the smoke set suggested, so three
in four pages leave the device under the shipped configuration. Absent A6 at
scale, that 75.7 percent figure, not the LLM-specific exposure the cascade
does verifiably reduce, is the number a reader takes away from a paper whose
second contribution is a disclosure control. It determines whether a
genuinely lower-disclosure operating point exists at all, not just how
precisely the current one is measured, and it is among the highest-value
items remaining in this list.

\textbf{Replace the sensitive-content predicate.} The adversarial slice has now
been expanded from twenty to forty-seven pages and rebalanced across
euphemism, non-English, outside-vocabulary and single-ambiguous-term cases
(\S\ref{sec:res-audit}); the false-negative rate went up, not down, once the
easier categories stopped diluting it. What remains is the fix: a model-based
detector, plausibly the entailment tier already resident in the extension,
scoring sensitivity hypotheses directly against this same slice.

\textbf{Improve loop synthesis.} The crossfade-window sweep and seam-minimising
loop-point selection proposed here have now been run (\S\ref{sec:res-loop},
Table \ref{tab:crossfade-sweep}); the second in particular warrants a
production change, pending confirmation at the scale of the full 141-clip
population rather than the 11-clip secondary sample tested here. The
clip-length study proposed here has also now been run on both halves of the
question (Tables \ref{tab:clip-length} and \ref{tab:duration-scaling}):
retention and seam quality are cell-dependent rather than a clean function of
requested duration, generation cost rises mildly superlinearly with it, and
together they give a mild case for a shorter default with no quality
counter-argument against one. The larger opportunity is conditioning the
generator to produce loop-closed material directly.

\textbf{Close the remaining measurement gaps.} The end-to-end in-browser
latency run is now done (Section~\ref{sec:res-e2e-latency}) and its
extraction/decision/decode segments still need to be plotted onto
Fig.~\ref{fig:f1} itself, not just reported separately in
Table~\ref{tab:e2e-latency}. The cold-cache re-run is the most overdue item
here: every generation-latency figure in this paper is a cache-hit or a
stubbed number,
and the paper currently has no measurement at all of the one path --
cache-miss, live generation in the browser -- that decides how badly the
fallback-then-swap design is actually needed. It should be run before, not
alongside, the other items in this paragraph: a batching measurement, a GPU
configuration, and an extraction re-run with the real in-browser embedding
rather than a stub, the last of which was attempted during this work but
blocked on a harness gap of its own, detailed in that section's notes. The 2 s
cache-check anomaly should be diagnosed first, since it dominates the common
path and would otherwise contaminate the cold-cache numbers this item is
meant to produce.

\textbf{Repeat the signal ablation under the live cascade.} The offline
per-signal ablation (\S\ref{sec:res-signal-ablation}) found that category
never changes and that behaviour and the embedding move mood on zero pages,
but it was run where every page is decided by a
keyword default or a fixed rule rather than by either model tier. That bounds
what colour and the embedding can be contributing rather than measuring their
contribution under the shipped zero-shot and LLM tiers. Repeating the
ablation with those tiers live is the direct follow-up, and, given that two
of the four signals have not yet been shown to move any output under any
condition, it is also the experiment that would tell us whether to keep
carrying behaviour and the embedding at all.

\textbf{Extend beyond English and beyond the page.} The language gate is the
common cause of two separate failures and deserves a dedicated treatment.
Beyond that, the same descriptor-to-profile machinery would apply to other
continuous contexts, such as documents, code editors or reading applications,
where the design questions of latency hiding, disclosure control and abstention
recur unchanged.
